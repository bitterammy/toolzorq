How Many Square Feet Is One Marla? Pakistan Land Area, Kanal and Acre Explained

Short answer: one marla is 272.25 square feet in official land records, but many housing societies use a smaller marla of 225 square feet. One kanal is always 20 marla, so it is 5,445 sq ft on the land-record standard and 4,500 sq ft on the housing-society standard. Always check which marla your plot uses before you agree a price.

If you are buying a plot or a house in Pakistan, the first number you meet is the marla. Listings say "5 marla", "10 marla" or "1 kanal", and the price is usually quoted per marla. The trouble is that a marla is not one fixed size. The same "10 marla" plot can be 2,722.5 square feet or 2,250 square feet depending on which marla the seller is using — a difference of almost 500 square feet, roughly the size of a bedroom. What is a marla?

A marla is a traditional unit of land area used in Pakistan and in parts of India. It comes from the old revenue system standardised during British rule, when land was measured with a chain and a rod rather than a tape measure. The standard marla is defined as a square 16.5 feet on each side — 16.5 × 16.5 feet — which works out at exactly 272.25 square feet. It is also 30.25 square yards, because one square yard is 9 square feet, and about 25.29 square metres.

The marla sits in the middle of a chain of traditional units that still appear on land records. One marla is made of 9 sarsahi, and each sarsahi is 30.25 square feet. Above the marla, 20 marla make 1 kanal, 8 kanal make 1 acre, and 25 acres make 1 murabba. In Punjab, an acre of farmland is also commonly called a killa. These units survive because land records, property listings and everyday conversation in Pakistan still use them — a plot is a "5 marla" plot long before anyone mentions its size in square feet.

For everyday use, remember three numbers: 1 marla is 272.25 sq ft on the land-record standard, 1 kanal is 20 marla, and 1 acre is 160 marla. Everything else in this guide is built from those three.

Why one marla is not always the same size

The confusing part is that Pakistan has two marla standards in everyday use, and both are called simply "marla". The first is the traditional revenue marla of 272.25 square feet, the one used in official land records, the fard (the record of ownership) and rural property. The second is the urban marla of 225 square feet, a square 15 feet on each side, which many housing societies and developers adopted because it fits neatly into planned street grids — a 225 sq ft marla makes a 30 × 75 ft plot exactly 10 marla, for example, where the revenue marla would give an awkward 8.26 marla.

The difference is about 17%: the urban marla is roughly 17% smaller than the revenue marla. That is not a rounding error; it is real land, and it costs real money. A "10 marla" plot measured with the revenue marla is 2,722.5 square feet; the same "10 marla" measured with the urban marla is 2,250 square feet. If you pay per marla without knowing which marla is being used, you can pay the same price for nearly 500 square feet less land. This is the single most common and most expensive misunderstanding in Pakistani property buying, and it is why every conversion in this guide is shown under both standards.

There is no single rule that tells you which marla a given listing uses. Some societies use the 225 sq ft marla, some use the 272.25 sq ft marla, and a few older settlements use a 250 sq ft marla. The only reliable answer is the one written on your plot's own documents — the allotment letter, the plot file, or the fard — or confirmed by the society's office. Never assume, and never take a seller's word for it without checking the paper.

Check the marla before the price. Two plots can both be "10 marla" and differ by 472.5 square feet. Before you compare prices, convert both plots to square feet with the same marla — or better, compare the price per square foot, which is the same under either standard.

Marla to square feet, square yards and square metres

The conversion itself is simple multiplication. Square feet equal marla × 272.25 on the revenue standard, or marla × 225 on the urban standard. To go the other way, divide square feet by 272.25 or by 225. Square yards are square feet divided by 9, and square metres are square feet multiplied by 0.092903.

The 5 marla and 10 marla rows are the ones most buyers need, because those are the standard residential plot sizes in Pakistani housing societies. A 5 marla plot is 1,361.25 sq ft on the revenue standard and 1,125 sq ft on the urban standard — a gap of 236.25 sq ft. A 10 marla plot is 2,722.5 sq ft against 2,250 sq ft, a gap of 472.5 sq ft. If you are comparing two plots of the same marla size, the gap between the standards is the first thing to check, because it is bigger than most rooms in the house you plan to build.

Square yards come up constantly in listings and in conversation — "gaj" is the everyday word — so it helps to know the yard figures too. One marla is 30.25 sq yd on the revenue standard and 25 sq yd on the urban standard. A 10 marla plot is therefore 302.5 sq yd or 250 sq yd. Square metres matter when you are reading an architect's drawing or a building plan, which is usually drawn in metric: one revenue marla is about 25.29 sq m, and one urban marla is about 20.90 sq m.

Kanal: 20 marla, two different sizes

One kanal is always 20 marla — that part never changes. What changes is how many square feet those 20 marla add up to. On the revenue standard, 1 kanal is 20 × 272.25 = 5,445 sq ft (605 sq yd, about 505.86 sq m). On the urban standard, 1 kanal is 20 × 225 = 4,500 sq ft (500 sq yd, about 418.06 sq m). The gap is 945 sq ft, which is larger than many whole apartments.

The kanal table also shows why the kanal is a useful unit for larger plots: a 2 kanal plot is a quarter of an acre on the revenue standard, and a 4 kanal plot is half an acre. But the same warning applies as with marla — a "1 kanal" house in one society can sit on 5,445 sq ft of land and a "1 kanal" house in another on 4,500 sq ft, and the houses themselves can look identical from the street. The plot file, not the listing headline, tells you which one you are buying.

Acre, killa and murabba

Above the kanal, the unit most people meet is the acre. One acre is 43,560 square feet, or 4,840 square yards. On the revenue standard, that is exactly 8 kanal or 160 marla, because 160 × 272.25 = 43,560. On the urban standard the arithmetic is different — 8 kanal at 225 sq ft per marla is 36,000 sq ft, which is not an acre at all — so for farmland and large plots, the revenue standard is the one that matters, and the acre is defined by it.

In Punjab, an acre of agricultural land is also called a killa, and 25 killa — 25 acres — make 1 murabba, which is 1,089,000 square feet. These larger units almost never appear in housing-society listings, but they are everywhere in farmland deals and in older land records, where a holding might be described as "3 murabba" rather than 75 acres. If you see killa or murabba on a document, convert them through the acre: 1 killa is 1 acre (43,560 sq ft), and 1 murabba is 25 acres (1,089,000 sq ft).

For scale, an acre is roughly the size of a football field without the end zones, and a single marla is roughly the size of a small bedroom or a single-car garage. A 5 marla plot is about the size of a small house footprint with a little yard; a 1 kanal plot is a generous family house with a lawn; and an acre is a small farm or a very large estate. Holding those pictures in mind makes the numbers easier to sense-check: if a "1 kanal" plot looks barely bigger than a 10 marla plot next door, one of the two is probably measured with the other marla.

Worked examples: the plot sizes people actually buy

Example 1 — a 5 marla house plot. On the revenue standard, 5 × 272.25 = 1,361.25 sq ft. On the urban standard, 5 × 225 = 1,125 sq ft. The difference is 236.25 sq ft. If the plot is priced at 2,000,000 PKR, that is 400,000 PKR per marla either way — but per square foot it is about 1,469 PKR on the revenue standard and about 1,778 PKR on the urban standard. Same price, same "5 marla" label, very different value per square foot.

Example 2 — a 10 marla plot. On the revenue standard, 10 × 272.25 = 2,722.5 sq ft (302.5 sq yd). On the urban standard, 10 × 225 = 2,250 sq ft (250 sq yd). The difference is 472.5 sq ft. A common 10 marla plot shape is 30 ft wide by 75 ft deep, which is 2,250 sq ft — exactly 10 urban marla, but only about 8.26 revenue marla. If the seller calls that plot "10 marla", they are using the urban marla, and you should price it as 2,250 sq ft, not 2,722.5.

Example 3 — a 1 kanal plot. On the revenue standard, 20 × 272.25 = 5,445 sq ft. On the urban standard, 20 × 225 = 4,500 sq ft. A typical 1 kanal plot is 50 ft × 90 ft, which is 4,500 sq ft — exactly 1 urban kanal, but only about 16.5 revenue marla. Again, the dimensions on the ground decide which marla is in play, and the plot's length and width are the quickest check you have.

Example 4 — working out marla from length and width. Suppose a plot measures 35 ft by 65 ft. Multiply to get the area: 35 × 65 = 2,275 sq ft. Divide by the marla you are checking: 2,275 ÷ 272.25 = 8.36 revenue marla, and 2,275 ÷ 225 = 10.11 urban marla. So a plot of that size is a little over 10 urban marla, or about 8 and a third revenue marla. This is the calculation to run whenever a listing gives dimensions instead of marla — and whenever a listing gives marla but no dimensions, ask for the dimensions and run it yourself.

Example 5 — comparing two plots fairly. Plot A is "10 marla" in a society using the revenue marla, priced at 8,000,000 PKR. Plot B is "10 marla" in a society using the urban marla, priced at 7,500,000 PKR. Plot B looks cheaper, but convert both to square feet: Plot A is 2,722.5 sq ft, so it costs about 2,939 PKR per sq ft; Plot B is 2,250 sq ft, so it costs about 3,333 PKR per sq ft. Plot A is actually the cheaper land. Comparing price per marla across societies with different marla is meaningless; comparing price per square foot is the only fair comparison.

How to check which marla your plot uses

There are five reliable checks, and you should use at least two of them before you pay anything:

  1. Read the plot file or allotment letter. The society's own paperwork usually states the plot dimensions in feet — for example "30 × 75" — and sometimes the marla standard as well. Multiply the dimensions yourself and divide by 272.25 and by 225 to see which one gives a round number.
  2. Check the fard. The fard, the record of ownership from the revenue department, uses the revenue marla of 272.25 sq ft. If the fard and the society's papers disagree, ask why — the disagreement itself is information.
  3. Ask the society's office, in writing. A verbal answer from an agent is not enough. Ask the housing society's office which marla standard applies to that block, and keep the written reply with your papers.
  4. Measure the plot. A tape measure and the plot's length and width settle the question in minutes. If the plot is 30 × 75 ft, it is 2,250 sq ft — 10 urban marla, not 10 revenue marla — whatever the listing says.
  5. Use the converter. TOOLZORQ's free Pakistan Land Area Converter lets you switch between the 225 and 272.25 standards and see the area in square feet, square yards and square metres at once, so you can check both readings side by side before you decide.

If the checks disagree with each other — the listing says one thing, the dimensions say another — stop and get the discrepancy explained before you sign anything. A genuine seller with genuine papers can explain it in one sentence. A seller who cannot explain it is a reason to walk away, not a reason to hurry.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Assuming every marla is 272.25 sq ft. It is the land-record standard, but many housing societies use 225 sq ft. Assuming the bigger number means overpaying by up to 17%.
  • Comparing price per marla across societies. Price per marla only compares like with like when both plots use the same marla. Convert to price per square foot first.
  • Confusing square yards with square feet. One square yard is 9 square feet, so 250 sq yd is 2,250 sq ft, not 250 sq ft. A listing in square yards looks smaller than the same plot in square feet, and mixing the two up is an easy way to misjudge a plot by a factor of nine.
  • Forgetting the difference between plot area and covered area. The marla measures the plot, not the house built on it. Building rules limit how much of the plot can be covered — the house itself is always smaller than the plot, and the marla says nothing about the covered area.
  • Trusting a rounded number. "About 10 marla" on a listing can hide a plot that is really 9.2 marla or 10.8 marla. Always work from the measured dimensions in feet.
  • Ignoring the 250 sq ft marla. A few older settlements use a 250 sq ft marla. If the dimensions do not match either 225 or 272.25, ask whether a third standard is in use.
