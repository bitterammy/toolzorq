How to Calculate Your BMI in KG and CM — Formula, Worked Examples and Healthy Weight Table

Short answer: to calculate your BMI in kg and cm, divide your weight in kilograms by your height in metres squared. First convert your height from centimetres to metres by dividing by 100, square that number, then divide your weight by the result. A BMI of 18.5 to 24.9 is classed as a healthy weight.

Body Mass Index, or BMI, is one of the most widely used numbers in health. The formula looks simple, but the centimetre-to-metre conversion trips people up — one mistake there can move your result by several points. What is BMI?

BMI stands for Body Mass Index. It is a single number that relates your weight to your height, giving a rough indication of whether your weight is in a range generally associated with good health for your height. Devised in the 1830s by the Belgian statistician Adolphe Quetelet, it was later adopted by health organisations around the world because it needs only two measurements anyone can take at home.

Weight on its own tells you very little: 80 kg means something different for a person who is 160 cm tall than for one who is 190 cm tall. Dividing weight by height squared adjusts for that difference, so the result can be compared across people of different heights. So BMI is used as a screening measure, sorting people into broad categories — underweight, healthy weight, overweight, and obese. It is a screening tool, not a diagnosis, and a general guide, not medical advice: it cannot tell you how much of your weight is fat and how much is muscle, or how fit you are.

What is the BMI formula in kg and cm?

The BMI formula is:

BMI = weight in kilograms ÷ (height in metres × height in metres)

Divide your weight in kilograms by the square of your height in metres. The formula needs height in metres, not centimetres — that is the step most people get wrong, and putting 175 in instead of 1.75 makes your answer far too small. The full method in kg and cm is:

  1. Divide your height in centimetres by 100 to get metres. For example, 175 cm ÷ 100 = 1.75 m.
  2. Multiply your height in metres by itself to square it. For example, 1.75 × 1.75 = 3.0625.
  3. Divide your weight in kilograms by that squared number. For example, 70 ÷ 3.0625 = 22.9.

That final number, 22.9, is the BMI — the number itself is the result, and you read it against the WHO categories in the table further down this guide.

Remember the conversion first. Always divide your centimetres by 100 before you square: squaring the centimetre figure by mistake makes the answer roughly ten thousand times too small.

How to calculate your BMI step by step

Step 1: Weigh yourself in kilograms. Use bathroom scales on a hard, flat floor, at the same time of day — a morning reading is a good standard. If your scales show pounds, the BMI Calculator accepts feet, inches, and pounds directly.

Step 2: Measure your height in centimetres. Stand straight against a wall with your heels, back, and head touching it, mark the top of your head, and measure from the floor to the mark.

Step 3: Convert centimetres to metres, then square the result. Divide your height by 100: 170 cm becomes 1.70 m, and 163 cm becomes 1.63 m. Keep both decimal places — rounding to the nearest ten centimetres can shift your BMI by a whole point. Then multiply the metre figure by itself: 1.70 × 1.70 = 2.89, and 1.63 × 1.63 = 2.6569.

Step 4: Divide your weight by the squared height. Divide your weight in kilograms by the number from Step 3, rounding to one decimal place — 22.9, not 22.857142. That is the whole calculation: convert, square, divide.

Worked examples: BMI calculated step by step

Each example follows the same three steps — convert, square, divide — rounded to one decimal place.

Example 1: 70 kg at 175 cm — BMI 22.9, healthy weight

175 cm ÷ 100 = 1.75 m. Square it: 1.75 × 1.75 = 3.0625. Divide the weight: 70 ÷ 3.0625 = 22.9, which falls in the healthy weight category of 18.5 to 24.9.

Example 2: 85 kg at 170 cm — BMI 29.4, overweight

170 cm ÷ 100 = 1.70 m. Square it: 1.70 × 1.70 = 2.89. Divide the weight: 85 ÷ 2.89 = 29.4, which falls in the overweight category of 25 to 29.9, just below the obese category.

Example 3: 55 kg at 160 cm — BMI 21.5, healthy weight

160 cm ÷ 100 = 1.60 m. Square it: 1.60 × 1.60 = 2.56. Divide the weight: 55 ÷ 2.56 = 21.5, which falls in the healthy weight category.

Example 4: 95 kg at 180 cm — BMI 29.3, overweight

180 cm ÷ 100 = 1.80 m. Square it: 1.80 × 1.80 = 3.24. Divide the weight: 95 ÷ 3.24 = 29.3, which falls in the overweight category — height makes a real difference to what a given weight means.

The boundaries matter. A BMI of 24.9 is healthy weight; 25.0 is overweight. A BMI of 29.9 is overweight; 30.0 is obese. Close to a boundary — 24.8, 25.1, 29.8 — check again with careful measurements: a small error can move you across the line. These categories are population-level guides, not a verdict on any one person's health.

What is a healthy weight for my height?

The healthy weight range for any height is the range of weights that gives a BMI between 18.5 and 24.9: the lower end is 18.5 multiplied by your height in metres squared, and the upper end is 24.9 multiplied by the same number. What should you do with your BMI result?

If your BMI is in the healthy range, the goal is to stay there: regular movement, balanced meals, and a re-check every few months.

If your BMI is below 18.5, you are underweight for your height. That can be natural, but it can also signal not eating enough, illness, or a digestive problem. If you have lost weight without trying, or feel tired, weak, or often unwell, speak to a doctor rather than fixing it alone.

If your BMI is between 25 and 29.9, you are in the overweight category. Small, steady changes work best: smaller portions, fewer sugary drinks, and more daily walking. Re-check your BMI every month or two.

If your BMI is 30 or above, you are in the obese category, and speak to a doctor, who can look at the full picture — blood pressure, blood sugar, cholesterol, and family history — and help you plan. BMI alone never justifies a crash diet.

What BMI does not tell you

It does not distinguish muscle from fat. Muscle is denser than fat, so a muscular person can have a high BMI while carrying very little body fat. A rugby player or weightlifter may be classed as overweight by BMI while lean and fit. For athletes, waist size or body fat percentage is more informative.

It does not say where fat is carried. Fat around the waist carries more health risk than fat on the hips and thighs, but BMI treats both the same.

It fits less well for older adults. As people age, they tend to lose muscle and height. An older person can have a healthy-range BMI while carrying more body fat and less muscle than the number suggests.

It does not apply to children or during pregnancy. Children are still growing, so their BMI is judged against age-and-sex percentile charts, interpreted by a doctor or health visitor. Weight changes during pregnancy are expected and healthy, so the adult categories do not apply then.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate my BMI in kg and cm?

Divide your height in centimetres by 100 to get metres, square that number, then divide your weight in kilograms by the result. For example, at 70 kg and 175 cm: 175 ÷ 100 = 1.75, 1.75 × 1.75 = 3.0625, and 70 ÷ 3.0625 = 22.9.

What is a healthy BMI?

For adults, the World Health Organization classes 18.5 to 24.9 as a healthy weight. Below 18.5 is underweight, 25 to 29.9 is overweight, and 30 and above is obese.

What is the healthy weight for 170 cm?

For a height of 170 cm, the healthy weight range is 53.5 kg to 72.0 kg. That comes from multiplying 1.70 squared (2.89) by 18.5 and by 24.9.
