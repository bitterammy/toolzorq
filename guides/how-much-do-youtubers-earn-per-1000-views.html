How Much Do YouTubers Earn Per 1,000 Views? RPM Explained with Worked Examples

Short answer: most long-form YouTubers earn roughly $1 to $6 per 1,000 views, with a median RPM of about $2.30. So 100,000 views at $2.30 RPM earns about $230, and 1 million views earns about $2,300. Shorts pay far less, roughly $0.02 to $1.48 per 1,000 views.

Every new creator asks the same question: how much will I earn per 1,000 views? There is no single number. Two channels with the same view count can earn very different amounts, because YouTube does not pay a fixed rate per view. It pays a share of what advertisers spend to reach a particular audience, and that changes with the topic, the country, the season and the video type.

This guide explains RPM and CPM, gives you a simple formula, the typical RPM ranges by niche, and worked examples for your own channel. It also covers the YouTube Partner Program, what moves your rate up or down, and how to use TOOLZORQ's free YouTube Money Calculator. Every figure here is a range or an estimate, never a guarantee.

What RPM and CPM actually mean

RPM stands for revenue per mille — "mille" is Latin for a thousand — and it is what a creator actually keeps per 1,000 views, after YouTube has taken its share. It counts every view, including ones that showed no ad. CPM stands for cost per mille: what advertisers pay per 1,000 ad impressions, before YouTube's cut. For long-form videos, creators keep 55% of the ad revenue and YouTube keeps 45%; for Shorts, creators keep 45% of their allocation from the Shorts Creator Pool. That split is already reflected in RPM, so RPM is the number to use for estimating your earnings.

TermWhat it measuresWhose number it is
RPMWhat a creator keeps per 1,000 views, after YouTube's shareThe creator's earnings
CPMWhat advertisers pay per 1,000 ad impressions, before YouTube's cutThe advertiser's cost

The formula: how to work out your earnings

The maths is simple once you know your RPM: monthly views × RPM ÷ 1,000 = estimated monthly earnings. You divide by 1,000 because RPM is a rate per thousand views. With 100,000 views and an RPM of $2.30, that is 100 thousands of views times $2.30, which comes to $230.

The hard part is knowing which RPM to use. A creator who plugs in a CPM of $20, or someone else's RPM, gets a figure that has nothing to do with their own channel. Use the ranges in this guide as a starting point, then replace them with your real RPM from YouTube Studio (Analytics, then Revenue). Until then, work with a range rather than a single number.

Typical RPM ranges by niche and country

Your niche is the biggest influence on your RPM. A study by AIR Media-Tech, based on verified YouTube Studio data from 300 channels between May 2025 and May 2026, found a median RPM of about $2.30 per 1,000 views, with niche rates from about $0.33 for kids' content to about $10.22 for education and science. Typical long-form RPM sits roughly between $1 and $6 per 1,000 views.

NicheTypical long-form RPM
Kids' contentAbout $0.33 per 1,000 views
Entertainment and gamingLow end, roughly $1–$2
General / mixed contentAround the $2.30 median
Finance, business, technologyHigher end, roughly $5–$10
Education and scienceUp to about $10.22

Country matters almost as much as niche. Advertisers in the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada and Australia generally pay more per ad impression, so the same video earns more per 1,000 views when its audience is there. Time of year matters too: advertisers spend more before Christmas, so RPMs often rise in November and December and fall in January, when budgets reset.

Long-form videos vs Shorts

Shorts pay far less than long-form videos. Shorts RPM is roughly $0.02 to $1.48 per 1,000 views depending on niche, according to AIR Media-Tech's study of 274 channels in September 2026 — roughly 3% to 14% of long-form rates. YouTube's own hypothetical example works out at $405 per 1 million engaged Shorts views in one country, about $0.41 per 1,000 views. That does not make Shorts worthless: they bring in subscribers quickly, so most creators use Shorts to grow the audience and long-form videos to earn from it. Keep the two formats separate in your estimates.

Who can earn: the YouTube Partner Program and getting paid

Views alone do not pay anything. To earn ad revenue, a channel must join the YouTube Partner Program (YPP): 1,000 subscribers plus either 4,000 valid public watch hours in the last 12 months, or 10 million valid public Shorts views in the last 90 days. Once in the programme, payment runs through AdSense, which pays monthly once earnings reach the $100 threshold — earn $60 in a month and you are paid nothing that month, because the $60 carries over until the balance reaches $100.

What moves your RPM up or down

RPM is not fixed, even for one channel. It moves with your audience, your topic and the advertising market. The main influences are:

  • Your niche. Finance, business, education and technology attract higher-paying advertisers than entertainment, gaming or kids' content.
  • Your audience's country. Viewers in the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada and Australia generally bring higher RPMs.
  • Video length and watch time. Longer videos can carry more ads, including mid-roll ads, so each view has more chances to earn.
  • Viewer age and income. Advertisers pay more to reach adults with spending power than to reach children.
  • Season. RPMs often rise before Christmas and fall in January, when advertising budgets reset.
  • Advertiser demand. If few advertisers are bidding on your topic in a given month, your RPM falls even if your views stay the same.
  • Ad blockers and Premium viewers. Views that show no ad earn no ad revenue.

Most of these are outside a creator's direct control. The realistic way to raise RPM is to pick a topic advertisers value and make videos people watch to the end. Stuffing a video with ads usually backfires: viewers leave and watch time falls.

Mistakes that inflate expectations

Most disappointment about YouTube earnings comes from the same few mistakes:

  • Using CPM instead of RPM. CPM is what advertisers pay before YouTube's cut, so it is always higher — estimating with it can double or triple your expected earnings on paper.
  • Counting views that cannot earn. Views before joining the Partner Program, views with no ad shown, and Shorts views estimated at long-form rates all inflate the total.
  • Trusting a single viral screenshot. One unusually good month, or a finance channel's RPM applied to a gaming channel, tells you nothing about your own rate.
  • Forgetting the $100 payout threshold. Earnings below $100 in AdSense are not paid out that month — they carry over until the balance reaches $100.
  • Assuming views equal income. A million views at a $0.33 kids' RPM earns about $330, while 100,000 views at a $15 RPM earns $1,500.
  • Treating an estimate as a guarantee. Any calculator, including TOOLZORQ's YouTube Money Calculator, gives a range based on the RPM you enter. If the RPM is wrong, the answer is wrong too.

The fix for all six is the same: estimate with RPM, use a range rather than a single number, and check your real RPM in YouTube Studio as soon as you can. Creators who do that are rarely surprised by their first payment; creators who do not are almost always disappointed, because the estimate was built on the wrong number.

Frequently asked questions

How much do YouTubers earn per 1,000 views?

Most long-form YouTubers earn roughly $1 to $6 per 1,000 views, with a median RPM of about $2.30. At that median, 100,000 views earns about $230 and 1 million views about $2,300.

What is the difference between RPM and CPM?

CPM is what advertisers pay per 1,000 ad impressions, before YouTube takes its cut. RPM is what a creator keeps per 1,000 views, after YouTube's share. RPM is always lower than CPM, and it is the number to use for estimating your own earnings.

How much do YouTube Shorts pay per 1,000 views?

Shorts pay roughly $0.02 to $1.48 per 1,000 views depending on niche — about 3% to 14% of long-form rates. YouTube's own hypothetical example works out at $405 per 1 million engaged Shorts views. Shorts are best for growth and subscribers, not for income per view.

How many subscribers and watch hours do I need to earn money?

You need to join the YouTube Partner Program: 1,000 subscribers plus either 4,000 valid public watch hours in the last 12 months or 10 million valid public Shorts views in the last 90 days. AdSense then pays monthly once your earnings reach the $100 threshold.

Why do two channels with the same views earn different amounts?

Advertisers pay different amounts to reach different audiences. Niche, audience country, viewer age, season and advertiser demand all change the RPM. A finance channel with a United States audience can earn several times more per 1,000 views than an entertainment channel with the same view count.

Can a calculator tell me exactly what I will earn?

No. A calculator like TOOLZORQ's YouTube Money Calculator shows an earnings range based on the RPM you enter, and that RPM is an estimate until your channel is earning. Your real RPM is in YouTube Studio under Analytics, then Revenue.
