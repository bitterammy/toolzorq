Most of the time, "how old am I?" needs only a year: subtract your birth year from the current year, and take one off if your birthday has not happened yet. But forms, school cut-offs, retirement rules and benefit schemes often want more than that. They ask for your age in years, months and days, or your age on a fixed date that is not today. At that point the simple subtraction stops working, because months are not all the same length and some years have an extra day. What "exact age" means

Your exact age, sometimes called your chronological age, is the time that has passed since your date of birth, expressed as completed years, then completed months, then days. "Completed" is the important word. You are 36 years old only once your 36th birthday has arrived. The day before it, you are still 35, however close the birthday is. The same rule applies to months: you are 4 months past your last birthday only once four whole calendar months have passed since it.

That is different from the age most people carry in their heads. We round up casually — "I'm nearly 37" in the month before a birthday — but official ages never round up. A school that admits children who are 5 years old on 1 September means children who have completed 5 years by that date, not children who will turn 5 during the autumn. A retirement rule that starts at 65 means 65 completed years. Knowing the exact figure matters whenever a date, not a feeling, decides the answer.

Exact age has three parts, and each part is counted in calendar units, not in averages. A year is a calendar year, from one birthday to the next. A month is a calendar month, from the same day-number in one month to the same day-number in the next. A day is a calendar day. No step in the calculation divides by 30 or by 365, which is why the answer is exact instead of approximate.

The method: three steps from birth date to today

Work forwards from the date of birth to the date you are measuring to. There are only three steps, and they always run in the same order.

  1. Whole years. Add years to the birth date for as long as you stay on or before the target date. The count of years you added is the completed years. The date you land on is your most recent birthday.
  2. Whole months. From that birthday, add whole calendar months while you still stay on or before the target date. The count of months you added is the completed months.
  3. Days. Count the days left between the date you reached in step 2 and the target date. That is the remaining days.

Here is the method on a real example. Take someone born on 15 June 1990, measured on 13 November 2026. Step 1: add years from 15 June 1990. After 36 years you reach 15 June 2026, which is on or before 13 November 2026. Adding a 37th year would reach 15 June 2027, which is past the target date, so stop: 36 completed years. Step 2: from 15 June 2026, add whole months. 15 July is 1 month, 15 August is 2, 15 September is 3, 15 October is 4. Adding a fifth month would reach 15 November, which is past 13 November, so stop: 4 completed months. Step 3: from 15 October to 13 November is 29 days — 16 days left in October (16 to 31 October) plus 13 days in November. The exact age is 36 years, 4 months and 29 days.

The same method works for any target date, not only today. Replace "today" with any date — a school cut-off, a retirement date, a date in the past — and the three steps give the exact age on that date. That is the whole method. Everything else in this guide is about the cases where the three steps need a rule to handle them.

Why subtracting the years is not enough

The shortcut everyone learns first is to subtract the birth year from the current year. For someone born in 1990 measured in 2026, that gives 36. Sometimes that is the right answer, and sometimes it is one too many: if the birthday has not happened yet this year, the correct age is 35. The fix is one extra check — subtract one more if this year's birthday has not arrived yet. That gives the completed years, which is step 1 of the method above.

But the year subtraction gives only the years. It says nothing about the months and days, and the months are where most mistakes happen. A common shortcut is to count the days since birth and divide by 365, or by 365.25 to allow for leap years. Both shortcuts are approximate, and both can be wrong by a day — which is exactly the kind of error that matters when a cut-off date decides something.

Take the first example: 13,300 days from 15 June 1990 to 13 November 2026. Divide by 365.25 and you get 36.41 years. That rounds to 36, which happens to be right, but the ".41" tells you nothing reliable about the months and days: turning a fraction of a year into months and days requires assuming a month length, and months are 28, 29, 30 or 31 days. The error comes from the leap days: this person has lived through 9 leap days (29 February in 1992, 1996, 2000, 2004, 2008, 2012, 2016, 2020 and 2024), and a fixed divisor of 365.25 spreads those extra days evenly across every year instead of putting them where they actually fall.

The failure is sharpest near a birthday, exactly where accuracy matters most. Measure the same person on 14 June 2026, the day before their 36th birthday. They have lived 13,148 days. Divide by 365.25 and you get 35.997 years — which rounds to 36, a whole year too many, when the true age is still 35. Truncate instead of rounding, and the fraction converts to about 11 months and 29 days, one day short of the true 11 months and 30 days. Count calendar years, months and days instead, and the answer is exact.

Leap years: the rule in full

A leap year is a year with 366 days instead of 365, with the extra day added as 29 February. The rule has three parts, and all three matter.

  • Divisible by 4: a year is a leap year if it divides evenly by 4. 2024 was a leap year; 2028 will be one.
  • Except centuries: a year divisible by 100 is not a leap year, even though it divides by 4. 1900 was not a leap year, and 2100 will not be one.
  • Unless divisible by 400: a year divisible by 400 is a leap year after all. 2000 was a leap year, and 2400 will be one.

The reason is astronomical: the Earth takes about 365.2425 days to orbit the Sun, not 365. Adding a day every four years overcorrects slightly, so the century rule takes a day back, and the 400 rule puts one back for the roundest centuries. The Gregorian calendar, which almost the whole world uses, follows exactly this rule.

For age calculations, leap years matter in two places. First, they add days to the total: someone born on 15 June 1990 has lived through 9 leap days by 13 November 2026, so their total is 13,300 days — nine more than the 13,291 days the same dates would span if leap days did not exist. Second, they matter for people born on 29 February, who have a birthday that only exists every four years — the next section covers that case.

The awkward cases: 29 February and the 31st of a month

Two birthdays need a rule, because the calendar does not always have a matching date to land on.

Born on 29 February

Someone born on 29 February 2000 has a birthday that exists only in leap years. In ordinary years there is no 29 February, so when do they turn a year older? There are two conventions, and different places use different ones. The common convention in everyday counting, and the one most calculators use, is that the birthday falls on 1 March in non-leap years: the person born on 29 February 2000 turns 26 on 1 March 2026. The other convention, used in some legal contexts, is 28 February. Both are defensible, and the difference is a single day, but it matters if a cut-off falls exactly on that boundary — a school or a benefits office will have its own rule, so check with them rather than assuming.

What is not in doubt is the day count. From 29 February 2000 to 28 February 2026 is 9,496 days, and to 1 March 2026 is 9,497 days. The total days are exact either way; only the label of the birthday moves by a day.

Born on the 31st of a month

Someone born on 31 January faces a similar problem every February, April, June, September and November, which have fewer than 31 days. The common rule is the same as for 29 February: if the target month is too short for the birth day, the last day of that month is used. Someone born on 31 January 2023 is one month old on 28 February 2023 (or 29 February in a leap year), and two months old on 31 March. The 28 days from 31 January to 28 February count as a full month, even though February is short.

This rule is why step 2 of the method says "add whole calendar months while you stay on or before the target date" rather than "add 30 days". Adding 30 days to 31 January gives 2 March, which is wrong; adding one calendar month gives 28 February, which is right. The calendar, not a fixed number of days, decides what a month is.

Your age on a specific date

Exact age is often needed for a date that is not today. Three uses come up most often.

How old will I be? Someone born on 1 January 2000 will be exactly 30 years old on 1 January 2030 — 10,958 days after birth, including 8 leap days (2000, 2004, 2008, 2012, 2016, 2020, 2024 and 2028). Asking "how old will I be in 2030?" is one of the most common age questions, and the method is the same: run the three steps with 1 January 2030 as the target date.

How old was I? The same method works backwards. A form may ask your age at the time of an event — when you started a job, when a contract was signed, when you moved house. Put the event's date in as the target date and the three steps give your age then. If the target date is before your birth date, there is no age to calculate, and the Age Calculator says so rather than giving a negative number.

Cut-off dates. Schools, sports leagues and benefit schemes often ask for your age on a fixed date, not on the day you apply. A school that admits children who are 5 on 1 September, a league with an under-18 cut-off on 1 January, or a pension that starts at a set age on a set date — all of these are "age on a date" questions. Run the three steps with the cut-off as the target date, and the completed years are the answer.

Total days, weeks and months lived

Alongside the years, months and days, three totals are useful. The total days lived is the plain count of calendar days between the birth date and the target date, leap days included. For the first example it is 13,300 days — nine more than the 13,291 days the same dates would span if leap days did not exist. The total completed months is the years times 12 plus the remaining months: 36 × 12 + 4 = 436, with 29 days already spent in the 437th month. The total weeks is the total days divided by 7: 13,300 days is exactly 1,900 weeks, while 12,993 days is 1,856 weeks and 1 day.

One more number people ask for is the days to your next birthday. For someone born on 15 June, measured on 13 November 2026, the next birthday is 15 June 2027, which is 214 days away — 17 days left in November, 31 in December, 31 in January, 28 in February 2027, 31 in March, 30 in April, 31 in May, and 15 in June. Common mistakes when working out your age

Forgetting the birthday check. Subtracting the birth year from the current year gives an age that is one too high for everyone whose birthday has not happened yet this year. Always ask: has this year's birthday arrived? If not, subtract one.

Dividing days by 365. Counting the days since birth and dividing by 365 ignores leap years, so it drifts by about a quarter of a day each year. Near a birthday, that drift can show the wrong whole number of years. Divide by 365.25 and the average is better, but it still fails on some dates, because leap days fall on specific dates rather than spreading evenly.

Treating every month as 30 days. A month is a calendar month, and months have 28, 29, 30 or 31 days. Adding 30 days to 31 January gives 2 March, but one calendar month after 31 January is 28 February. The fixed-30-day shortcut is wrong at every month boundary it crosses.

Guessing the 29 February birthday. People born on 29 February turn a year older on 1 March in ordinary years under the common convention, or on 28 February under the convention some legal contexts use. Do not assume which one applies to your situation — check with the school, office or scheme that asked for the age.

Mixing up age and age on a date. "How old am I?" and "how old will I be on 1 September?" are different questions with different answers. If a form gives a cut-off date, calculate the age on that date, not the age today. The Age Calculator's "Age on date" option exists for exactly this.

Counting the birth day twice. When counting days between two dates, the start date itself is day zero, not day one. Someone born on 15 June 1990 is 0 days old on 15 June 1990 and 1 day old on 16 June. Counting the birth date as day one adds a day to every total.
