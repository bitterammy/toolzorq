Most of us choose a pack by its shelf price. The $4.80 jar looks cheaper than the $5.76 jar, so the $4.80 jar goes in the trolley. That habit costs money every week, because shelf prices compare totals, not value. A jar that costs more in total can easily cost less per gram — and the only number that tells you which pack is genuinely cheaper is the unit price: what one kilogram, one litre, or one item actually costs. What is unit price?

Unit price is the cost of one standard unit of a product. If a bag of rice costs $2.40 per kilogram, the unit price is $2.40 per kg. If a bottle of shampoo costs $0.30 per 100 ml, the unit price is $0.30 per 100 ml. The formula is always the same:

Unit price = total price ÷ quantity

Once two packs are expressed in the same unit, the comparison is trivial: the lower number wins. That is the whole method, and it works for anything sold by weight, volume, count, or even by use — price per wash for laundry detergent, price per sheet for toilet paper, price per serving for cereal. The unit you choose does not matter, as long as it is the same unit for both packs. Comparing $0.20 per ounce with $0.20 per gram is meaningless, because an ounce and a gram are different sizes; comparing $5.00 per kg with $4.50 per kg is a real comparison.

Shops know this, which is why many supermarkets print the unit price in small type on the shelf label. But shelf labels are inconsistent: one brand's label may show price per kg while the next shows price per 100 g, and some labels are missing altogether. Working it out yourself — or with a calculator — takes seconds and removes the guesswork.

Why the shelf price misleads you

Supermarkets are designed around the bigger-pack assumption: bigger looks like better value, and usually it is. But "usually" is doing a lot of work there. Brands sometimes price the large pack at a premium per unit, counting on shoppers who assume bigger is cheaper and never check. Promotions make it worse. A small pack on special offer can beat the family pack's everyday price, and a "value pack" that is mostly packaging can cost more per gram than the standard size.

Take a real example. Coffee A costs $6.00 for 250 g. Coffee B costs $10.50 for 500 g. The shelf prices say A is cheaper. The unit prices say otherwise: A costs $6.00 ÷ 0.25 kg = $24.00 per kg, while B costs $10.50 ÷ 0.5 kg = $21.00 per kg. B is cheaper by $3.00 per kg, or 12.5%. If you buy a kilogram of coffee a month, choosing B saves you $36 over a year — for the same coffee, from the same shelf, with no coupon and no loyalty card. The saving is invisible until you divide.

The same logic applies in the other direction. A 32 oz jar for $5.76 looks more expensive than a 24 oz jar for $4.80, yet the larger jar costs $0.18 per ounce against the smaller jar's $0.20 per ounce. The jar that costs more at the till costs less per spoonful. Unit price is the only fair way to compare packs of different sizes, and it is the reason the calculator exists.

How to calculate unit price by hand

You do not need a calculator for a simple comparison, and knowing the steps helps you trust the tool's answer. The method has three steps.

  1. Read the price and the size off each pack. Use the net weight or volume printed on the label — the amount of product inside, not the weight of the packaging. For a multi-pack, multiply: a 12-pack of 16 oz bottles is 192 fl oz in total, not 16 oz.
  2. Put both packs in the same unit. If one pack is in grams and the other in kilograms, convert one to match the other. The conversions you will use most are: 1 kg = 1,000 g, 1 litre = 1,000 ml, 1 lb = 16 oz, 1 oz = 28.35 g, 1 kg = 35.27 oz, and 1 US gallon = 128 fl oz.
  3. Divide price by quantity for each pack, then compare. The lower result is the better buy. To see how much cheaper it is, subtract the lower unit price from the higher one, divide by the higher one, and multiply by 100 to get the percentage saving.

Multi-packs, discounts, and special offers

Real shopping is messier than two plain packs, and the adjustments are straightforward once you know them. A multi-pack is one quantity: a 12-pack of 500 ml bottles is 6 litres in total, so divide the price by 6 litres, not by 500 ml. A buy-one-get-one-free offer doubles the quantity while the price stays the same: a 400 g pack at $4.00 with a free second pack is 800 g for $4.00, which is $5.00 per kg. A coupon or instant rebate comes off the price before you divide: a 500 g pack at $5.00 with a $1.00 coupon costs $4.00 for 500 g, which is $8.00 per kg. For a percentage discount, multiply the price by what remains after the discount — a $10.00 pack at 20% off costs $8.00 — and then divide as usual.

Loyalty prices work the same way: use the price you will actually pay at the till, not the headline price. And if a promotion requires buying two to get the discount, count the full quantity you must take home, because the second pack is part of the cost even if you did not want it. The rule for every adjustment is the same: the price is what you actually pay, the quantity is what you actually receive, and the division happens after both are settled.

When the cheapest per unit is not the best buy

Unit price answers one question — which pack costs least per unit — and it is the wrong question when the cheapest pack is one you will not finish. Five kilograms of rice at $2.00 per kg beats one kilogram at $2.40 per kg only if you use all five kilograms before it goes stale. If half of it ends up thrown away, the effective price is $10.00 for 2.5 kg used, which is $4.00 per kg — far worse than the small pack. The same applies to anything perishable, anything that goes off once opened, and anything you simply will not use: the cheapest unit price in the shop is expensive if half the product ends up in the bin.

Storage is the second limit. A bulk pack that does not fit in your cupboard, your fridge, or your freezer is not a bargain; it is clutter you paid for. Cash flow is the third: tying up money in a six-month supply of detergent saves a little per wash and costs you the use of that money in the meantime. And quality still matters. Two packs with the same unit price are not the same product if one is concentrated and the other is diluted, if one lists 40 washes per bottle and the other lists 25, or if one is a brand you like and the other is one you will not finish. For concentrated products, compare by the finished quantity — price per wash, price per serving — rather than by bottle size.

The practical rule is simple: use unit price to find the cheapest pack among the ones you will actually use up, in a size you can store, before it expires. That is the comparison that saves money; the rest is arithmetic without judgement.

Unit price around the shop

The method is the same everywhere, but the right unit changes with the aisle. In the grocery aisle, weight and volume rule: price per kg for rice, flour, and coffee; price per litre for oil, milk, and juice. For counted products, use price per item: per roll for toilet paper and paper towels, per sheet if the roll sizes differ, per nappy, per capsule for dishwasher tablets. For cleaning and laundry products, price per wash is often fairer than price per litre, because a concentrated formula delivers more washes from a smaller bottle — read the wash count off the label and divide by it. For pet food, price per kg is the honest unit, and for bulk buys it is worth checking the cost per day: a 15 kg bag at $45.00 is $3.00 per kg, but if your dog eats 300 g a day, that is $0.90 a day, and a smaller bag at a worse unit price may still be the right buy if the big bag will go stale.

Outside groceries, the same division works. Hardware sold by length or area — price per metre, price per square foot — compares directly once the units match. Printer paper compares by price per sheet, not price per ream, because reams come in different sheet counts. Common mistakes when comparing unit prices

Comparing different units. This is the most common error: $0.20 per ounce against $0.20 per gram looks like a tie, but an ounce is 28.35 grams, so the per-ounce pack is nearly thirty times more expensive per gram. Always convert first, then divide.

Using gross weight instead of net weight. The weight on the front of the pack includes the product only when it says net weight. Shipping weight, which includes the box and packaging, inflates the quantity and makes the unit price look better than it is. Use the net weight printed on the nutrition or contents panel.

Comparing different products. A unit price only compares like with like. A premium coffee at $21.00 per kg and a budget coffee at $12.00 per kg are different products; the cheaper one is better value only if you would happily drink it. The same applies to different grades, blends, and concentrations.

Forgetting the count inside a multi-pack. A 6-pack at $5.40 is $0.90 per item only if there are six items inside. Some "packs" contain fewer items than the box suggests, and some contain more. Count the items, multiply, and divide by the total.

Ignoring the shelf label entirely. Where a shelf label does show a unit price, use it as a cross-check: if your calculation and the label disagree by a lot, re-read the pack size. The label is usually right, and a large gap usually means you read the wrong number — a drained weight instead of a net weight, or a per-100-g figure read as per-kg.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate unit price?

Divide the total price by the quantity: unit price = price ÷ quantity. A $4.50 pack of 1 kg costs $4.50 per kg; a $2.50 pack of 500 g costs $5.00 per kg. Put both packs in the same unit before you divide, then pick the lower result.

Is the bigger pack always cheaper per unit?

No. A bigger pack is often cheaper per unit, but not always — brands sometimes price the large size at a premium, and a small pack on promotion can beat the family pack. That is exactly what the Unit Price Calculator checks: it compares the price per kg, litre, or item, not the shelf price.

What unit should I compare in?

Use the unit that matches how the product is sold and used: per kg or per gram for food sold by weight, per litre or per ml for liquids, per item for counted products, and per wash or per serving for concentrated products. The unit does not matter as long as it is the same for both packs.

How do I compare packs in different units, like grams and kilograms?

Convert one to match the other before you divide: 1 kg = 1,000 g and 1 litre = 1,000 ml. TOOLZORQ's Unit Price Calculator does this automatically — it converts weight to grams and volume to millilitres as its base units — and the Unit Converter handles any other conversion.

Does unit price work with discounts and buy-one-get-one-free offers?

Yes. Use the price you actually pay and the quantity you actually receive: subtract a coupon from the price before dividing, and double the quantity for a buy-one-get-one-free offer. A 400 g pack at $4.00 with a free second pack is 800 g for $4.00, which is $5.00 per kg.

Which currency does the calculator use?

Any currency. Enter the prices in your own currency and the comparison works the same way, because both packs are compared in that currency. The result is a price per unit and a percentage saving, not a currency conversion.

Rule of thumb: divide price by size in the same unit, and buy the lower number — among the packs you will actually finish. A 500 g pack at $2.50 is $5.00 per kg; a 1 kg pack at $4.50 is $4.50 per kg, so the bigger pack wins by 10%, but only if you use the whole kilogram.

Quick checklist

  • Have you read the net weight or volume off each pack, not the gross or shipping weight?
  • Are both packs in the same unit — grams with grams, litres with litres, items with items?
  • Have you counted the total quantity in a multi-pack before dividing?
  • Have you used the price you will actually pay, after coupons, loyalty prices, and discounts?
  • Have you checked the saving in percent, not just in shelf price?
  • Will you actually finish the cheaper pack before it expires, and can you store it?
  • Are you comparing the same product — same grade, same concentration, same quality?
