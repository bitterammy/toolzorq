How to Work Out Your Hourly Wage as a Yearly Salary

Short answer: multiply your hourly rate by the hours you work each week, then by the weeks you work each year. For a full-time job — 40 hours a week for 52 weeks — that is your hourly rate × 2,080. So $20 an hour is $41,600 a year, $25 an hour is $52,000 a year, and $30 an hour is $62,400 a year, all before taxes. Hourly pay and salary are two ways of describing the same money, but they are surprisingly hard to compare in your head. A job offer of $25 an hour sounds modest next to a salary offer of $52,000 a year, yet they are the same pay for a full-time schedule. A $2-an-hour rise sounds small until you learn it is worth more than $4,000 a year. And a part-time job at a high hourly rate can pay less over a year than a full-time job at a lower one, because the hours matter as much as the rate. This guide explains the formula, works through the conversion tables people actually look up, covers part-time hours, unpaid time off, overtime and the difference between gross pay and take-home pay, and shows how to compare an hourly offer with a salary offer fairly. The formula: hourly rate × hours × weeks

The conversion has three parts, and every one of them matters:

Yearly salary = hourly rate × hours per week × weeks per year

The hourly rate is what you are paid for one hour of work. The hours per week are the hours you actually work — 40 for a standard full-time job, fewer for part-time work, more if you regularly work overtime. The weeks per year are the weeks you are actually paid for — 52 for a full year, fewer if you take unpaid time off. Multiply the three together and you get your yearly pay before taxes, known as gross pay.

For a standard full-time schedule the formula collapses into a single number that is worth memorising: 2,080. That is 40 hours a week × 52 weeks, the number of hours in a full-time working year. To convert any full-time hourly rate to a yearly salary, multiply the rate by 2,080:

  • $15 an hour × 2,080 = $31,200 a year
  • $20 an hour × 2,080 = $41,600 a year
  • $25 an hour × 2,080 = $52,000 a year
  • $30 an hour × 2,080 = $62,400 a year

The 2,080 figure assumes you are paid for every week of the year, including holidays and paid vacation. That is the usual assumption behind salary figures quoted by employers: a $52,000 salary is normally understood as the pay for a full year including paid time off, not 52 weeks of work with no holidays. If your job does not pay for holidays or vacation, use the number of weeks you are actually paid for instead — the worked examples below show how.

There is also a quick mental shortcut that gets you close in seconds: double the hourly rate and add three zeros. $25 an hour becomes roughly $50,000 a year; $30 an hour becomes roughly $60,000. The shortcut slightly underestimates the true figure — $25 an hour is exactly $52,000, not $50,000 — because a year has 2,080 working hours, not 2,000. It is good enough for a first look at a job advert, but use the exact figure before you accept an offer or compare two jobs.

Monthly, biweekly, weekly and daily pay — and the "× 4" trap

Once you have the yearly figure, the other pay periods follow from simple division — but only if you divide by the right number:

  • Monthly pay = yearly pay ÷ 12. $52,000 a year is $4,333.33 a month.
  • Biweekly pay (every two weeks) = yearly pay ÷ 26. $52,000 a year is $2,000 every two weeks. There are 26 biweekly pay periods in a year, and two months of the year contain three paychecks instead of two.
  • Weekly pay = yearly pay ÷ 52. $52,000 a year is $1,000 a week.
  • Daily pay = weekly pay ÷ days worked per week. On a five-day week, $1,000 a week is $200 a day.

The trap is multiplying weekly pay by 4 to get monthly pay. It feels natural — four weeks in a month — but it is wrong, because most months have more than four weeks. At $20 an hour, weekly pay is $800. Multiply by 4 and you get $3,200 a month, but the true monthly figure is $3,466.67 — $800 a week × 52 weeks ÷ 12 months. The shortcut undercounts by $266.67 a month, which is $3,200 over a year: a whole month of pay disappears. The same error works in reverse if you multiply a monthly salary by 12 and divide by 52 to get weekly pay — that one is correct, because it goes through the yearly figure. The rule is simple: always go through the yearly number, and divide by 12 for a month, 26 for two weeks, and 52 for a week.

The biweekly figure matters for budgeting, because a biweekly paycheck arrives 26 times a year, not 24. If you are paid every two weeks, ten months of the year bring two paychecks and two months bring three. People who budget on two paychecks a month and treat the third paycheck in those two months as a bonus are using the calendar correctly — the extra paychecks are real, and they add up to a full month of pay over the year.

Working backwards: salary to hourly rate

Sometimes the offer is a salary and you want to know what it means per hour — to compare it with your current hourly job, or to work out what an hour of overtime or freelance work is worth. The conversion runs in reverse: divide the salary by the hours in the working year.

Hourly rate = yearly salary ÷ (hours per week × weeks per year)

For a full-time schedule, divide by 2,080:

  • $45,000 a year ÷ 2,080 = $21.63 an hour
  • $60,000 a year ÷ 2,080 = $28.85 an hour
  • $75,000 a year ÷ 2,080 = $36.06 an hour
  • $100,000 a year ÷ 2,080 = $48.08 an hour

The same warning about hours applies in reverse. If the salaried job regularly demands 50 hours a week rather than 40, the real hourly rate is lower than the 2,080 calculation suggests. A $60,000 salary at 50 hours a week for 52 weeks is $60,000 ÷ 2,600 = $23.08 an hour, not $28.85. Salaried roles often come with expectations that stretch beyond 40 hours, and the honest comparison uses the hours you will actually work, not the hours in the contract. That is one of the reasons an hourly job at $28 an hour can be a better deal than a $60,000 salary that expects long weeks: the hourly worker is paid for every extra hour, while the salaried worker is not.

Part-time hours and unpaid time off

The 2,080 figure only applies to a full-time, fully paid year. Change either the hours or the weeks and the answer changes with them. The formula stays the same — hourly rate × hours per week × weeks per year — you just put in your real numbers.

At $20 an hour, the yearly pay for different weekly hours is:

Unpaid time off cuts the weeks instead of the hours. Two unpaid weeks off at $25 an hour and 40 hours a week means 50 paid weeks instead of 52: $25 × 40 × 50 = $50,000, which is $2,000 less than the full $52,000. At $20 an hour, two unpaid weeks bring the year from $41,600 down to $40,000 — a $1,600 drop, which is exactly two weeks of pay at $800 a week. The general rule is easy to remember: each unpaid week costs one week of pay, and each week of pay is the hourly rate × hours per week.

This is also why comparing two hourly jobs by rate alone can mislead. A job at $22 an hour for 30 hours a week pays $34,320 a year ($22 × 30 × 52), while a job at $20 an hour for 40 hours a week pays $41,600. The lower rate wins by $7,280 a year, because it comes with ten more hours a week. Always multiply the rate by the hours before comparing offers — the rate is only one third of the formula.

Overtime: when the rate changes

Overtime breaks the simple formula, because the extra hours are paid at a higher rate. Under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act in the United States, most hourly workers who are not exempt must be paid at least one and a half times their regular rate for hours worked over 40 in a week — commonly called time and a half. Some states, employers and union agreements set more generous rules, and some jobs are exempt, but time and a half over 40 hours is the standard case.

Take a worker earning $20 an hour who regularly works 45 hours a week. The first 40 hours pay $20 an hour: 40 × $20 = $800. The extra 5 hours pay time and a half, which is $30 an hour: 5 × $30 = $150. Weekly pay is $950, not the $900 that a flat $20 rate would give. Over 52 weeks that is $49,400 a year — $7,800 more than the $41,600 that the straight 2,080 calculation gives. Five overtime hours a week are worth nearly 19% extra over the year.

Regular overtime changes the comparison between jobs, too. A $20-an-hour job with steady overtime can out-earn a $22-an-hour job with none: $49,400 a year against $45,760. But overtime is not guaranteed income — it depends on the employer's needs, and it can disappear. When you are comparing offers, count overtime you can reasonably expect, and treat anything beyond that as a bonus rather than a promise. Gross pay vs take-home pay

Every figure in this guide so far is gross pay: the amount before anything is taken out. Take-home pay — the amount that actually reaches your bank account — is always lower, and the gap can be surprisingly large. The deductions that create the gap are:

  • Federal income tax, which depends on your total income, your filing status and the deductions you claim. The United States uses progressive tax bands, so only the income above each threshold is taxed at the higher rate.
  • State and local income tax, which varies by where you live and work. Some states have no income tax at all; others take a significant share.
  • Payroll taxes (FICA): Social Security at 6.2% of wages and Medicare at 1.45%, a combined 7.65% taken from every paycheck, with the Social Security portion applying only up to an annual wage limit that changes from year to year.
  • Benefit deductions: your share of health insurance premiums, retirement plan contributions such as a 401(k), and any other deductions you have chosen.

As a rough guide, a single filer with no dependents and no pre-tax deductions might take home roughly 75% to 85% of gross pay after federal tax and FICA, before state tax — but that is only a rule of thumb, and the real figure depends on your circumstances. A $52,000 gross salary might mean roughly $3,200 to $3,700 a month in take-home pay rather than the $4,333 gross monthly figure. Never budget on the gross number: use your pay stub, or a paycheck calculator that asks for your filing status, state and deductions, to find your real take-home figure.

It is also worth knowing that a pay rise can push part of your income into a higher tax band without making you worse off. Because tax bands are progressive, only the income above the threshold is taxed at the higher rate — the rest of your pay is taxed exactly as before. A rise from $20 to $25 an hour is worth the full $10,400 a year before tax, and the extra tax applies only to the extra income, never to the pay you were already earning.

Hourly vs salary: comparing two job offers fairly

Converting the numbers is only half the comparison. An hourly job and a salaried job at the same yearly figure are not the same deal, because the pay structures come with different rules and different benefits. These are the differences that change the answer:

Overtime. Hourly workers who are not exempt are generally paid extra for hours over 40 a week. Salaried workers who are exempt are generally not — extra hours are part of the job. If a salaried role regularly needs 50-hour weeks, its effective hourly rate is a fifth lower than the 2,080 calculation suggests, as the worked example above shows. Ask how many hours the role really takes before you compare the headline figures.

Paid time off. Salaried roles usually include paid holidays and paid vacation, so the salary is paid in full even in weeks you do not work. Some hourly roles pay only for hours worked, so a holiday or a sick week means a smaller paycheck. When an hourly offer has no paid time off, subtract the unpaid weeks from the weeks in the formula — a $25-an-hour job with two unpaid weeks is $50,000 a year, not $52,000.

Benefits. Health insurance, retirement contributions, paid sick leave and other benefits have a real cash value, and they are often more generous in salaried roles. A salary offer of $50,000 with health insurance can be worth more than an hourly job paying $55,000 a year with no benefits, once you price the insurance you would have to buy yourself. Put a rough value on the benefits before you compare.

Income stability. A salary is the same every pay period, whatever the workload. Hourly pay varies with the hours: fewer hours in a slow week mean a smaller paycheck. That variability cuts both ways — it also means extra hours are paid — but if your bills are fixed, the predictability of a salary has a value of its own.

Pay rises. A $1-an-hour rise is worth $2,080 a year at full-time hours. A 3% rise on a $52,000 salary is worth $1,560 a year. The hourly rise is worth more in this case, because the base is the same $52,000 and 3% of it is less than the $2,080 a $1 hourly rise gives. Convert both rises to yearly figures before judging which is better.

None of this means one structure is always better. It means the conversion is the starting point, not the whole comparison: convert the pay, then adjust for overtime, paid time off and benefits, and only then decide.

Common mistakes when converting hourly pay to a salary

Using 2,080 for a part-time job. 2,080 hours is 40 hours a week for 52 weeks. A 30-hour week is 1,560 hours a year, and at $20 an hour that is $31,200, not $41,600. Use your real hours.

Forgetting unpaid time off. Two unpaid weeks at $25 an hour cost $2,000 a year. If your job does not pay for holidays or vacation, those weeks come out of the 52 before you multiply.

Multiplying weekly pay by 4 for monthly pay. A month averages 4.33 weeks, so weekly pay × 4 undercounts the month by a full week of pay over the year. Divide the yearly figure by 12 instead.

Comparing gross pay with take-home pay. A $52,000 salary and a $52,000 hourly equivalent are the same gross pay, but the take-home figure depends on tax, state and deductions. Compare gross with gross, and work out take-home separately.

Ignoring overtime in one offer and counting it in the other. Steady overtime at time and a half can add nearly a fifth to yearly pay. Count the overtime you can reasonably expect in both offers, or in neither — not in one alone.

Judging a job by its hourly rate alone. A higher rate at fewer hours can pay less over a year than a lower rate at full-time hours. $22 an hour for 30 hours a week is $34,320 a year; $20 an hour for 40 hours is $41,600. Always multiply before comparing.

Treating a salary as 2,080 hours when the job needs more. A $60,000 salary at 50 hours a week is $23.08 an hour, not $28.85. Use the hours the job actually takes, not the hours in the contract.

Frequently asked questions

How much is $20 an hour a year?

$41,600 a year before taxes, at 40 hours a week for 52 weeks — $20 × 2,080. That is $3,466.67 a month, $1,600 every two weeks and $800 a week. Take two unpaid weeks off and the total drops to $40,000.

How much is $25 an hour a year?

$52,000 a year before taxes at full-time hours — $25 × 2,080. That is $4,333.33 a month, $2,000 every two weeks and $1,000 a week. It is one of the most searched conversions because $52,000 is a common salary benchmark.

What is the formula to convert hourly pay to salary?

Yearly salary = hourly rate × hours per week × weeks per year. For a full-time job that is hourly rate × 2,080. To go the other way, divide the salary by 2,080: $60,000 a year is $28.85 an hour.

How do I work out my salary if I work part-time?

Use your real hours in the same formula. At $20 an hour for 25 hours a week, the year is $20 × 25 × 52 = $26,000. For 30 hours it is $31,200, and for 32 hours it is $33,280. The 2,080 shortcut does not apply to part-time work.

Is $20 an hour good pay?

It depends on where you live, your hours and your costs. $20 an hour full-time is $41,600 a year before taxes — above the federal minimum wage annual figure of $15,080, and roughly in line with the lower middle of full-time earnings in the United States. Whether it is good pay for you depends on your rent, your household size and the cost of living where you are.

How much is $30 an hour a year?

$62,400 a year before taxes at 40 hours a week for 52 weeks — $30 × 2,080. That is $5,200 a month, $2,400 every two weeks and $1,200 a week.

Does overtime count in the hourly to salary conversion?

Not in the basic formula, because overtime is paid at a higher rate — usually time and a half for hours over 40 a week. Work it out separately: at $20 an hour with 5 overtime hours a week, weekly pay is $950 instead of $800, and yearly pay is $49,400 instead of $41,600. Add the overtime to the calculator's result.

Why is my monthly pay not my weekly pay × 4?

Because a month averages 4.33 weeks, not 4. Weekly pay × 4 misses about a week of pay every three months. Divide the yearly figure by 12 for the true monthly amount: $800 a week is $3,466.67 a month, not $3,200.
