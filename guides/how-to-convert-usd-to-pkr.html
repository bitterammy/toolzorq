How to Convert USD to PKR Online — Today's Rate, the Formula, and What Your Bank Actually Pays

27 November 2026

Short answer: To convert USD to PKR, multiply the dollars by the current exchange rate: PKR = USD × rate. The rate moves every day, so there is no fixed answer. Check the live mid-market reference rate first — then expect your bank or transfer service to pay slightly less, because it adds a margin and fees.

People in Pakistan turn dollars into rupees every day: freelancers paid by clients abroad, families receiving money from relatives overseas, and shoppers buying from international websites. The question is always the same — how many rupees will I actually get? Three figures are in play at once: the rate you see on Google, the rate your bank offers, and the rate at an exchange counter. Every example below uses a clearly labelled example rate of 1 USD = 275 PKR — an example, not today's rate.

What is the USD to PKR exchange rate today?

There is no fixed answer, and any guide that prints a rate is out of date within hours. The rate between the US dollar (USD) and the Pakistani rupee (PKR) moves through the day as currencies are bought and sold, and shifts again overnight. That is why this guide states no rate anywhere: the only rate that matters is the one in force at the moment you convert.

When people ask for "today's rate", they usually mean the mid-market reference rate — the figure shown by Google and converter sites, and the basis of the daily reference rate published by the State Bank of Pakistan. It is a useful benchmark, but it is a reference, not a price you can trade at: no bank will hand you rupees at exactly that rate.

How do you convert USD to PKR? The formula

The conversion is one multiplication:

PKR = USD × exchange rate

Take the amount in US dollars, multiply it by the number of rupees one dollar buys, and the answer is the amount in Pakistani rupees. The difficulty is never the maths — it is knowing which rate to multiply by.

The examples below use an example rate of 1 USD = 275 PKR. It is not today's rate — check the converter for the current rate first.

  • 10 USD: 10 × 275 = 2,750 PKR.
  • 50 USD: 50 × 275 = 13,750 PKR.
  • 100 USD: 100 × 275 = 27,500 PKR.
  • 500 USD: 500 × 275 = 137,500 PKR.
  • 1,000 USD: 1,000 × 275 = 275,000 PKR.

The rate matters more than the arithmetic: a freelancer paid 800 USD a month receives 800 × 275 = 220,000 PKR at the example rate, but 224,000 PKR if the rate moves to 280 — a difference of 4,000 rupees for the same work. When a result has a fraction, round it to the nearest rupee.

What is the mid-market rate?

The mid-market rate is the midpoint between the price at which banks buy a currency from each other and the price at which they sell it. If banks buy dollars at 274 rupees and sell them at 276, the mid-market rate is 275. It is the rate you see on Google and converter sites.

Three things matter about it. First, it is a reference, not a deal: no bank trades with the public at the mid-market rate, because the gap between buying and selling prices is how banks earn their money. Second, it moves constantly while markets are open. Third, it is still the best benchmark you have — the closer a bank's rate is to it, the better the deal.

Why does your bank pay less than the rate you see online?

The answer has two parts: the spread and the fees. Both are normal, and both are easy to miss if you only look at the headline rate.

The spread is the gap between the rate at which a bank buys dollars from you and the rate at which it sells dollars to you. At our example mid-market rate of 275 PKR, a bank might buy your dollars at 272 rupees and sell dollars to you at 278. When you sell dollars you get the lower number; when you buy dollars you pay the higher one.

Fees are the second cut. A bank or transfer service may charge a flat fee, a percentage of the amount, or both, and the receiving bank may add its own charge. Fees are often shown separately from the rate, which is why comparing headline rates alone misleads.

How do you convert PKR back to USD?

Converting the other way — rupees back to dollars — uses the same rate, but you divide instead of multiplying:

USD = PKR ÷ exchange rate

At the example rate of 1 USD = 275 PKR, the reverse rate is 1 ÷ 275 = 0.00364 USD per rupee. Here are the reverse examples, all at the example rate of 275:

  • 10,000 PKR: 10,000 ÷ 275 = 36.36 USD.
  • 27,500 PKR: 27,500 ÷ 275 = 100 USD.
  • 55,000 PKR: 55,000 ÷ 275 = 200 USD.
  • 275,000 PKR: 275,000 ÷ 275 = 1,000 USD.

The reverse conversion is the one people use when paying for something in dollars from a rupee account — a subscription, a course, or a flight. Note that the bank's selling rate applies here, so 27,500 PKR buys slightly less than 100 USD at a real bank. As a check, convert the answer back the other way: if the two directions do not agree, you have mixed up the multiply and the divide.

Cash, bank transfer or a remittance service — which rate do you get?

Cash at an exchange counter. Counters display a buying rate and a selling rate on a board, and they deal in physical notes. The rate is usually close to the bank rate, and it can vary from one counter to another. Cash is immediate, but count the notes before you leave the counter.

Bank transfer. A transfer into a Pakistani bank account in dollars is converted by the receiving bank at its own rate — usually the mid-market rate minus the spread — plus any fee for receiving foreign currency. It is the safest route for larger amounts, but also the slowest.

Remittance service. A remittance service shows its rate and its fee up front, and tells you exactly how many rupees the recipient will get. That transparency is its main advantage: you can compare the final rupee figure across services in a minute. The only fair comparison is the final amount received — a service with a slightly worse rate and no fee can beat one with a better rate and a high fee. Whichever method you choose, ask for the same three numbers before you commit: the rate, the fee, and the final amount in rupees. Common mistakes when converting USD to PKR

Using an old rate. A rate from a screenshot or last week's message is history, not a price. Always check the live rate at the moment you convert.

Treating the Google rate as the amount you will receive. The rate Google shows is the mid-market reference rate. Your bank will pay you less — that is the spread, not an error.

Forgetting the fee. A flat fee of a few hundred rupees is easy to overlook on a large transfer and painful on a small one.

Mixing up the buy and sell rates. Banks publish two rates: one for buying dollars from you and one for selling dollars to you. When you are turning dollars into rupees, the buying rate is the one that applies to you.
