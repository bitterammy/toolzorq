How Many Calories Should I Eat Per Day? BMR, TDEE and Activity Levels Explained

Short answer: multiply your basal metabolic rate (BMR) by an activity factor to get your daily maintenance calories, also called your TDEE. A moderately active 30-year-old man, 180 cm and 80 kg, maintains his weight on about 2,759 calories a day. Eat about 500 fewer calories a day to lose roughly half a kilo a week, or about 500 more a day to gain.

Most people who start watching their weight have no idea how many calories they should actually eat — so they guess, pick a round number from the internet, and then wonder why nothing changes. The honest way to answer the question is to work it out from your own body: first your basal metabolic rate, then your activity level, then your goal. This guide shows you how to calculate how many calories you need a day, step by step, with worked examples you can check, tables by age and sex, and the maths behind TOOLZORQ's free Calorie Calculator, which runs entirely in your browser with no sign-up.

General guide, not medical advice. The numbers in this guide come from the published Mifflin-St Jeor equation and standard activity multipliers. They are estimates for healthy adults, and no calculator can replace a clinician. If you have a health condition, are pregnant or breastfeeding, are under 18, or are under treatment, talk to a professional before changing what you eat.

What is BMR?

BMR stands for basal metabolic rate: the energy your body burns at complete rest, just to stay alive. Even lying still, your body spends energy on breathing, circulating blood, keeping your temperature steady, repairing cells and running your organs. BMR is usually the biggest single part of what you burn in a day — for a desk worker who barely moves, it can be most of the total.

BMR depends mainly on four things: your weight, your height, your age and your sex. Bigger bodies burn more at rest, because there is more tissue to maintain. BMR falls slowly with age — the formula subtracts 5 calories a day for every year of life, so two otherwise identical people ten years apart differ by about 50 calories a day. Men usually have a higher BMR than women of the same size, because the formula gives men a small addition and women a subtraction, reflecting average differences in muscle mass.

One thing BMR does not include is movement. It is the resting figure only. If you eat exactly your BMR and do nothing all day, you would slowly lose weight, because even getting out of bed and walking to the kitchen burns energy on top of it. That is why the next step — activity — matters so much.

How do I work out my BMR?

The calculator on this site uses the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, published in 1990 and widely regarded as one of the most accurate simple formulas for healthy adults. It needs only your weight in kilograms, your height in centimetres, your age in years and your sex:

Men: BMR = 10 × weight (kg) + 6.25 × height (cm) − 5 × age + 5

Women: BMR = 10 × weight (kg) + 6.25 × height (cm) − 5 × age − 161

Work through it in order. Take a man aged 30, 180 cm tall and 80 kg:

10 × 80 = 800. 6.25 × 180 = 1,125. 5 × 30 = 150. Then 800 + 1,125 − 150 + 5 = 1,780 calories a day. That is his BMR: what his body burns at complete rest.

Now a woman aged 30, 165 cm tall and 65 kg:

10 × 65 = 650. 6.25 × 165 = 1,031.25. 5 × 30 = 150. Then 650 + 1,031.25 − 150 − 161 = 1,370 calories a day (1,370.25, rounded down). The same height, weight and age would give a man 1,536 — the difference is the +5 for men against the −161 for women, a gap of 166 calories a day built into the formula.

Older formulas exist — the Harris-Benedict equation, first published in 1919 and revised in 1984, is the best known — but they tend to overestimate for modern bodies, which is why the Mifflin-St Jeor equation is the usual choice today. Different equations disagree by roughly 50 to 100 calories a day on the same person, which tells you something useful: any BMR figure is an estimate with a margin of error, not an exact measurement. Treat it as a starting point, then adjust it by what actually happens to your weight over a few weeks.

What is TDEE and how do I work it out?

TDEE stands for total daily energy expenditure: everything you burn in a typical day, including movement. It is your maintenance calories — eat about this much and your weight stays roughly steady; eat less and you lose weight; eat more and you gain. TDEE is simply your BMR multiplied by an activity factor:

TDEE = BMR × activity factor

Activity levelFactorWhat it means
Sedentary1.2Desk job, little or no exercise
Lightly active1.375Light exercise or sports 1–3 days a week
Moderately active1.55Moderate exercise or sports 3–5 days a week
Very active1.725Hard exercise or sports 6–7 days a week
Extra active1.9Very hard exercise, a physical job, or training twice a day

These multipliers are the standard ones used with the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, and they are the same figures TOOLZORQ's Calorie Calculator uses. The activity factor is the least precise part of the whole calculation, because it depends on an honest judgement of your own week. Most people with a desk job and a few workouts a week are lightly active, not moderately active — picking too high a level is the most common reason a calorie target comes out too generous. If you sit most of the day and exercise three times a week, use 1.375, not 1.55.

Notice how much the factor matters. The same BMR of 1,780 gives 2,136 calories a day at sedentary and 3,382 at extra active — a difference of 1,246 calories, larger than a whole meal. Getting the activity level right matters more than getting the BMR exactly right.

Worked example 1: a 30-year-old man, 180 cm, 80 kg

His BMR is 1,780 calories a day, worked out above. Multiply it by each activity factor to see his maintenance calories at every level:

To lose weight, subtract about 500 a day from the maintenance figure: at moderate activity that is 2,759 − 500 = 2,259 a day. To gain weight, add about 500: 2,759 + 500 = 3,259 a day. The reasons for the 500 figure are explained below.

To lose weight steadily she would aim for about 1,384 a day (1,884 − 500). To gain, about 2,384 a day (1,884 + 500). Notice that her weight-loss target, 1,384, is above the 1,200-a-day floor that health guidance commonly treats as the minimum for women without medical supervision — if your own calculation ever drops below that floor, do not simply eat less; talk to a clinician first.

Second, the activity gap is far bigger than the age gap: at any age, moving from sedentary to moderately active adds about 450 to 650 calories a day to what you can eat without gaining. If your target feels too low to live with, adding activity is usually a better answer than cutting food further.

These are reference bodies, not your body. Your own height and weight move the numbers — every 5 kg more adds about 50 calories a day to BMR, and every 5 kg less takes the same off. For your own figure, use the calculator rather than this table.

How many calories should I eat to lose weight?

Take your maintenance calories and subtract. The standard advice is about 500 calories a day below maintenance, which adds up to 3,500 calories over a week. A kilogram of body fat stores roughly 7,700 calories, so a 3,500-calorie weekly deficit works out at about 0.45 kg — roughly a pound — of fat loss a week. That pace is the one health agencies commonly describe as sustainable: fast enough to see progress, slow enough to keep muscle and energy.

To lose half a kilo a week, the deficit needs to be about 550 calories a day (7,700 × 0.5 ÷ 7 = 550). The round 500-a-day figure is close enough to use in practice, and the difference between 500 and 550 is smaller than the error in any calorie estimate. A bigger deficit loses weight faster at first, but it also costs more muscle, more hunger and more fatigue, and it is harder to keep going — most people who cut very hard end up quitting. A steady 500-a-day deficit that you can hold for months beats a 1,000-a-day deficit that lasts a week.

Three guardrails apply. First, do not go below about 1,200 calories a day for women or 1,500 for men without medical supervision — below those levels it becomes hard to get enough nutrients, and the risks rise. Second, recalculate as you lose weight: a lighter body has a lower BMR, so a target that worked at 90 kg will be too generous at 80 kg. Re-run the numbers every 5 kg or so. Third, expect the scale to move unevenly. Water retention, glycogen and the timing of meals can hide a week's fat loss, so judge progress over two to three weeks, not day by day.

Food choices matter as much as the number. Two diets at the same calories are not equally easy to follow: protein and fibre keep you full longer than the same calories from sugar or refined starch, and liquid calories — soft drinks, juice, milky coffees — add up fast without ever making you feel fed. The calculator gives you the budget; what you spend it on decides whether you can stick to it.

How many calories should I eat to gain weight?

The same maths works in reverse: add about 500 calories a day above maintenance to gain roughly half a kilo a week. For the moderately active 30-year-old man in the first example, that means about 3,259 calories a day (2,759 + 500). For the lightly active woman in the second example, about 2,384 a day (1,884 + 500).

What you gain depends on what you do while you eat more. With strength training, a good share of the gain is muscle; without it, most of it is fat. That is why people gaining weight deliberately usually pair the surplus with resistance training several times a week, and why they keep the surplus modest — 500 a day, not 1,500. A very large surplus adds fat much faster than it adds muscle, and the extra fat then has to be dieted off again.

If you struggle to eat enough, the practical fixes are the mirror image of the weight-loss advice: eat more often, choose energy-dense foods such as nuts, oils, dried fruit and full-fat dairy, and drink some of your calories — a smoothie or a milky drink is easier to finish than the same calories as solid food. As with weight loss, recalculate as your weight changes, because a heavier body burns more.

Common mistakes when working out your calories

Picking too high an activity level. This is the biggest error, and it inflates the answer by hundreds of calories. A desk job plus three gym sessions a week is lightly active, not moderately active. Count only real exercise, not a busy day.

Treating BMR as the daily target. BMR is what you burn at complete rest. Eating your BMR and living a normal day puts you in a deficit, because every step, chore and workout burns energy on top of it. The target is TDEE, not BMR.

Using the same number forever. Your BMR falls as you lose weight and as you age. A target calculated at 90 kg is too generous at 80 kg. Re-run the calculation every few kilos or every few months.

Mixing up units. The Mifflin-St Jeor equation needs kilograms and centimetres. If you weigh yourself in pounds, divide by 2.205 to get kilograms; if you measure height in feet and inches, convert to centimetres first (1 inch = 2.54 cm). The calculator takes kg and cm directly, so enter those.

Trusting the number to the last calorie. The equation is an estimate, food labels carry rounding, and activity trackers routinely overstate how much exercise burns. A target of 2,259 is not meaningfully different from 2,300 — what matters is the trend over weeks, not the exact figure on day one.

How accurate is a calorie calculator?

About as accurate as any formula can be for a body it has never measured. The Mifflin-St Jeor equation was tested against measured metabolic rates and came out as one of the closest simple formulas for healthy adults, but individual bodies still vary: two people with the same age, height, weight and sex can differ by 100 calories a day or more, because the equation cannot see muscle mass, hormones or genetics. It also cannot see how much you actually move — the activity factor is a judgement, not a measurement.

The practical way to use the number is as a starting point. Eat at the calculated target for two to three weeks and watch the scale trend. If your weight is steady, the target is right. If you are losing faster than about half a kilo a week on a maintenance target, eat a little more; if you are gaining on a weight-loss target, eat a little less. The calculator gives you the first number; your own results give you the corrected one.

Frequently asked questions

How many calories should a 30-year-old man eat per day?

It depends on activity. A 30-year-old man, 180 cm and 80 kg, has a BMR of 1,780 calories a day: about 2,136 a day if sedentary, 2,448 if lightly active, 2,759 if moderately active, 3,071 if very active and 3,382 if extra active. Enter your own figures in TOOLZORQ's free Calorie Calculator for your number.

What is the difference between BMR and TDEE?

BMR is what your body burns at complete rest. TDEE is BMR multiplied by an activity factor, so it includes everything you burn in a typical day. TDEE is your maintenance calories — the number to eat at to keep your weight steady.

How many calories do I need to lose 1 kg?

About 7,700 calories of deficit in total, since a kilogram of body fat stores roughly that much energy. At a deficit of 500 calories a day, that takes about 15 days; at 550 a day, about 14 days — roughly half a kilo a week.

Is 1,200 calories a day enough?

For most adult women, 1,200 calories a day is about the lowest intake that health guidance commonly treats as safe without medical supervision; for men the corresponding floor is about 1,500. If your calculated weight-loss target falls below those levels, talk to a clinician rather than eating less.

Do I need to count calories every day?

Not forever. Counting for a few weeks teaches you what your usual meals actually contain, and after that many people can hold roughly the right intake without tracking every bite. If progress stalls, a week of careful counting usually shows where the extra calories are coming from.

Why does my calorie target change as I lose weight?

Because a lighter body burns less at rest: every 5 kg lost lowers BMR by about 50 calories a day, and the activity multiplier applies to that smaller BMR. Re-run the calculation every 5 kg or so, or your target will quietly become too generous.
