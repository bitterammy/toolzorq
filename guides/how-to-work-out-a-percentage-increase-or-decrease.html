How to Work Out a Percentage Increase or Decrease

Short answer: to work out a percentage increase, subtract the old value from the new value, divide the difference by the old value, and multiply by 100. A price rising from $80 to $92 is (92 − 80) ÷ 80 × 100 = 15%. The same formula gives a decrease: a negative answer means the value fell, so a price dropping from $120 to $96 is a 20% decrease. Percentage change is one of the most used calculations in everyday life, and one of the most often done wrong. A pay rise, a price increase, a discount, a fall in sales, an exam result — all of them are usually described as a percentage, not as a plain amount. The amount tells you how much changed; the percentage tells you how big that change is compared with where it started. A $10 rise means something very different on a $50 bill than it does on a $5,000 salary, and the percentage is what captures that difference. This guide explains the formula step by step, works through the examples people actually meet — pay rises, prices, discounts, sales, exam marks — and covers the mistakes that give wrong answers, including the ones that look right. What a percentage increase actually measures

A percentage increase measures a change relative to the starting value. That last part is the whole point of the calculation. Saying a price rose by $12 is a fact, but it is not a comparison: $12 on an $80 item is a large rise, while $12 on a $1,200 item is barely noticeable. The percentage turns the change into a share of the starting value, so changes of very different sizes can be compared on the same scale. A 15% rise is a 15% rise whether the item costs $80 or $8,000.

The starting value is therefore the denominator of the whole calculation — it is the thing the change is measured against. Every mistake in percentage change comes back to this point in one way or another: dividing by the new value instead of the old one, forgetting which value was the starting point, or comparing a change with the wrong baseline. Keep the starting value fixed in your mind and the rest of the calculation follows.

It also helps to know what a percentage increase is not. It is not the same as a percentage-point change, which is covered later in this guide. A rate moving from 10% to 15% has risen by 5 percentage points, but that is a 50% relative increase, because 5 is half of the starting 10. And a percentage increase is not the same as "what percent of the new value is the change" — that is a different question with a different answer, and mixing the two up is the single most common error in this calculation.

The formula and the three steps

The formula for percentage change is one line:

Percentage change = (new value − old value) ÷ old value × 100

A positive answer means an increase. A negative answer means a decrease. The same formula handles both, which is why there is no separate "percentage decrease formula" — a decrease is just a negative percentage change. The three steps are:

  1. Find the difference. Subtract the old value from the new value. This is the change in plain numbers: how much the value moved, and in which direction. If the new value is larger, the difference is positive. If it is smaller, the difference is negative.
  2. Divide by the old value. Divide the difference by the starting value — the old value, never the new one. This turns the change into a share of where it started: a decimal such as 0.15, which means the change was 15 hundredths of the starting value.
  3. Multiply by 100. Multiply the decimal by 100 to turn it into a percentage. 0.15 becomes 15%, and −0.20 becomes −20%, which you would report as a 20% decrease.

Work it once on a simple pair of numbers. A price rises from $80 to $92. Step 1: the difference is 92 − 80 = 12. Step 2: divide by the old value, 12 ÷ 80 = 0.15. Step 3: multiply by 100 to get 15%. The price rose by 15%. Now reverse the direction. A price falls from $120 to $96. Step 1: 96 − 120 = −24. Step 2: −24 ÷ 120 = −0.20. Step 3: −0.20 × 100 = −20%. The price fell by 20%.

One detail worth noticing: the units cancel out. Dollars, kilograms, points, visitors — whatever the values measure, the difference and the old value are in the same units, so the units divide away and the answer is a plain percentage. That is why the same formula works for a pay rise, a weight change and a sales report without any adjustment.

Worked examples: pay, prices, sales and marks

Four examples from the situations where people actually need this calculation, each worked through with the same three steps.

A pay rise

Annual pay rises from $48,000 to $57,600. Step 1: the difference is 57,600 − 48,000 = 9,600. Step 2: 9,600 ÷ 48,000 = 0.20. Step 3: 0.20 × 100 = 20%. The pay rise is 20%. Notice how the large numbers make the rise look big in plain dollars — $9,600 is a lot of money — while the percentage puts it in proportion: the pay is now one fifth higher than it was.

A price increase

A product previously cost $80 and now costs $92. Step 1: 92 − 80 = 12. Step 2: 12 ÷ 80 = 0.15. Step 3: 15%. The price rose by 15%. The same steps on a smaller rise: a coffee that cost $4.00 and now costs $4.50 has risen by 0.50, and 0.50 ÷ 4.00 = 0.125, which is 12.5%. Small prices can hide large percentages, which is why a percentage is the fair way to compare price rises across different items.

A price fall and a discount

A jacket is reduced from $120 to $96. Step 1: 96 − 120 = −24. Step 2: −24 ÷ 120 = −0.20. Step 3: −20%, which is a 20% decrease — the jacket is 20% off. The same pair of numbers in reverse shows the trap of going back: if the jacket later returns from $96 to $120, the increase is (120 − 96) ÷ 96 × 100 = 25%, not 20%. A 20% fall followed by a 20% rise does not get you back to the start, because the second change is measured against a smaller starting value. This is covered again in the mistakes section below.

Sales growth

Monthly sales rise from $24,000 to $30,000. Step 1: 30,000 − 24,000 = 6,000. Step 2: 6,000 ÷ 24,000 = 0.25. Step 3: 25% growth. A business reporting "sales up 25%" is making exactly this calculation. The same formula on a fall: sales dropping from $30,000 to $27,000 is (27,000 − 30,000) ÷ 30,000 × 100 = −10%, a 10% decrease.

Percentage points are not the same as percent

The most confusing pair of ideas in this whole area is percentage points versus percent, and they are confused constantly in news reports, bank letters and exam questions. When a value is already a percentage, a change in it can be described in two ways, and the two numbers are different.

Suppose an interest rate moves from 10% to 15%. The change is 5 percentage points: 15 − 10 = 5. But the relative change is (15 − 10) ÷ 10 × 100 = 50%: the rate is half as large again as it was. Saying "the rate rose by 5%" would be wrong — it rose by 5 percentage points, which is a 50% increase. The distinction matters whenever the starting value is itself a percentage: tax rates, interest rates, unemployment rates, pass rates, market shares.

A simple way to keep them apart: percentage points are the plain subtraction of two percentages, and percent is that subtraction divided by the starting value. If a politician says unemployment fell by 2 percentage points, from 8% to 6%, the relative fall is 25%, because 2 is a quarter of 8. Both statements are true; they describe the same change in different units. When a report gives only one of them, work out the other before deciding whether the change is large or small.

Adding a percentage and taking one off

So far the guide has worked out the percentage change between two known values. The reverse job — starting from a value and a percentage, and finding the new value — comes up just as often: add 15% to a price, take 20% off in a sale, add tax to a bill. For this, use a multiplier instead of the three-step formula.

To add a percentage, multiply by 1 plus the rate as a decimal. Adding 15% means multiplying by 1.15. A price of $80 with 15% added is 80 × 1.15 = $92. Adding 10% to 80 means 80 × 1.10 = $88. Adding 25% means multiplying by 1.25, so 80 becomes 100.

To take a percentage off, multiply by 1 minus the rate as a decimal. Taking off 20% means multiplying by 0.80. A price of $250 with 20% off is 250 × 0.80 = $200. Taking off 30% means multiplying by 0.70, so a $85 item becomes 85 × 0.70 = $59.50. Taking off 50% means multiplying by 0.50, which is halving the price.

Multipliers also make repeated changes easy. A 10% rise followed by another 10% rise is not a 20% rise: 100 × 1.10 × 1.10 = 121, so the combined increase is 21%. The second rise is measured against the already-risen value, so it adds slightly more than the first. The same compounding applies to discounts: two successive 10% discounts leave 100 × 0.90 × 0.90 = 81, which is a 19% total reduction, not 20%.

And a rise followed by an equal fall never returns to the start. Increase 100 by 20% and you get 120. Decrease 120 by 20% and you get 96 — four below where you started. The fall is 20% of a larger number, so it takes off more than the rise added. This is the mistake behind many "the price went up then came back down" stories: unless the percentage fall is smaller than the percentage rise, the value ends up lower than it began.

Working backwards: find the original value

Sometimes you know the final value and the percentage, and need the starting value. A sale price after a 20% discount, a salary after a 5% rise, a bill with tax already added. The fix is to divide by the multiplier instead of multiplying by it.

After a discount: a game costs $64 after a 20% discount. The multiplier was 0.80, so the original price is 64 ÷ 0.80 = $80. Check it: 80 × 0.80 = 64, which matches. The common mistake is to add 20% of $64, which gives 64 × 1.20 = $76.80 — wrong, because 20% of the sale price is smaller than 20% of the original price.

After a rise: a salary is $63,000 after a 5% rise. The multiplier was 1.05, so the original salary is 63,000 ÷ 1.05 = $60,000. Check it: 60,000 × 1.05 = 63,000. Again, subtracting 5% of the new figure would give the wrong answer, because the percentage was taken from the smaller original number.

After tax is added: a bill totals $115 and includes 15% tax. The multiplier was 1.15, so the pre-tax amount is 115 ÷ 1.15 = $100, and the tax is $15. The same rule works in reverse for any percentage that has already been applied: divide by the multiplier to undo it.

The general rule is simple: multiplying by the multiplier moves forwards, dividing by it moves backwards. If you know the starting value and the percentage, multiply. If you know the final value and the percentage, divide. Mixing those two up is the second most common error in percentage work, after dividing by the wrong value in the first place.

Three related calculations people mix up

Percentage change is one of three everyday percentage jobs, and the other two are often confused with it. All three are built into TOOLZORQ's Percentage Calculator as separate modes, because each one needs different numbers.

"What is X% of a number?" This asks for a share of a value, not a change in it. What is 20% of 150? Multiply 150 by 0.20 to get 30. What is 15% of 80? 80 × 0.15 = 12. This is the calculation behind tips, tax on a bill, commission on a sale, and the amount of a discount before you subtract it. It is not a percentage increase: 20% of 150 is 30, but a rise from 150 to 180 is a 20% increase — the same numbers can appear in both, which is why they get mixed up.

"What percentage is one number of another?" This is the marks calculation: 42 out of 50 is (42 ÷ 50) × 100 = 84%. It asks what share the first number is of the second, with no change involved at all. A student who scores 42 out of 50 has 84%; a student who scores 45 out of 60 has 75%. The same division, without subtracting anything first, is also how you work out what share of a total something represents: 30 out of 200 is 15%.

"What is the percentage change?" This is the calculation this guide is about: subtract first, then divide by the starting value. The subtraction is the step that separates it from the other two. If your numbers are a starting value and a new value, subtract. If your numbers are a score and a total, do not subtract — just divide. If you are finding a share of one number, multiply by the rate as a decimal.

Common mistakes when working out a percentage change

Dividing by the new value. A price rising from $80 to $92 is a 15% increase, because 12 ÷ 80 = 0.15. Dividing by the new value, 12 ÷ 92, gives about 13% — which is the increase as a share of the final price, not the percentage increase. Always divide by the value you started from.

Treating a rise and an equal fall as cancelling out. A 20% rise followed by a 20% fall leaves you below where you started: 100 → 120 → 96. The fall is taken from a larger number, so it removes more than the rise added. To get back to the start after a 20% fall, you need a 25% rise: 96 × 1.25 = 120.

Adding percentages that apply to different bases. Two successive 10% rises are a 21% rise in total, not 20%, because the second rise applies to the already-risen value. Percentages can only be added directly when they are shares of the same starting value.

Confusing percentage points with percent. A rate moving from 10% to 15% has risen by 5 percentage points, which is a 50% relative increase. Saying it rose by 5% understates the change tenfold. When the starting value is already a percentage, say which one you mean.

Saying "increased by 150%" when you mean "is 150% of". A value that rises from 40 to 100 has increased by 150%, because the change is 60 and 60 ÷ 40 = 1.5. But 100 is 250% of 40, not 150%. "Increased by" describes the change; "is …% of" describes the final value compared with the start. They differ by exactly 100 percentage points.

Calculating a percentage change from zero. If the old value is zero, the formula divides by zero, and the percentage change is undefined — there is no meaningful answer. A business going from 0 sales to 50 sales has grown, but not by any percentage. Report the plain numbers instead: from 0 to 50.

Forgetting the subtraction. Dividing the new value by the old value without subtracting first gives the ratio, not the change: 92 ÷ 80 = 1.15, which is 115%. That is the new value as a percentage of the old one. The percentage increase is 115% − 100% = 15%. Forgetting to subtract the 100% is the same as forgetting step 1 of the formula.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate a percentage increase?

Subtract the old value from the new value, divide the difference by the old value, and multiply by 100. From 80 to 92 is (92 − 80) ÷ 80 × 100 = 15%. Or enter both numbers in TOOLZORQ's Percentage Calculator in "Change from → to" mode and read the answer.

How do I calculate a percentage decrease?

Use the same formula: (new value − old value) ÷ old value × 100. The answer comes out negative, and the size of the negative number is the decrease. From 120 to 96 is (96 − 120) ÷ 120 × 100 = −20%, a 20% decrease.

What is the difference between a percentage increase and percentage points?

A percentage increase is a relative change: the difference divided by the starting value. Percentage points are the plain subtraction of two percentages. A rate moving from 10% to 15% rises by 5 percentage points, which is a 50% relative increase.

How do I add 15% to a number?

Multiply by 1.15. Adding 15% to 80 gives 80 × 1.15 = 92. To take 20% off, multiply by 0.80: 250 × 0.80 = 200. To undo a percentage that has already been applied, divide by the multiplier instead.

How do I work out my exam marks as a percentage?

Divide your score by the total and multiply by 100. A score of 42 out of 50 is (42 ÷ 50) × 100 = 84%. TOOLZORQ's Percentage Calculator has a "Marks: scored out of" mode for exactly this calculation.

Why does a 20% rise followed by a 20% fall not return to the start?

Because the fall is taken from the larger, risen value. 100 rises by 20% to 120, then falls by 20% of 120 — which is 24 — leaving 96. To return from 96 to 120 you need a 25% rise, because 24 is a quarter of 96.

What if the starting value is zero?

The percentage change is undefined, because the formula divides by zero. Report the plain numbers instead — for example, "sales rose from 0 to 50" — rather than inventing a percentage.

Quick checklist

  • Have you identified the old value — the starting point — and used it as the denominator?
  • Have you subtracted first, before dividing, so the answer is the change and not the ratio?
  • Is a negative answer a decrease, and have you reported it as one rather than as a negative increase?
  • If the value is already a percentage, have you said whether you mean percentage points or percent?
  • If you are adding or removing a percentage, are you multiplying by the right multiplier — 1.15 to add 15%, 0.80 to take off 20%?
