How Many Grams Are in a Cup of Flour? Cups to Grams for Baking, with the Full Chart

Short answer: one US cup of all-purpose flour weighs about 120 grams when it is spooned into the cup and levelled off — the figure King Arthur Baking publishes and the one TOOLZORQ's converter uses. The USDA lists 125 grams for the same cup, because it fills the cup slightly differently. A cup of granulated sugar is about 200 grams and a cup of butter is 227 grams. There is no single number for "a cup" because a cup measures volume and a gram measures weight — the answer depends on what is in the cup.

Every baker who has followed an American recipe has hit the same wall: the recipe says "2 cups of flour" and your kitchen scale only speaks grams. You cannot look up one number and use it for everything, because a cup of flour and a cup of sugar are the same size but very different weights. Flour is light and full of air; sugar is dense and packs down. The same cup holds about 120 grams of one and about 200 grams of the other — a difference of 80 grams, enough to change a cake from tender to tough.

This guide gives you the full picture: the chart of gram weights for every common baking ingredient, the simple formula behind every conversion, the spoon weights for small amounts, why published figures disagree, how the size of the cup itself changes the answer, and worked examples that convert whole recipes line by line. The weights here are the standard approximations TOOLZORQ's free Cups to Grams Converter uses, taken from King Arthur Baking and USDA density data — so the numbers in this guide and the numbers the tool gives you always agree.

Why is there no single answer?

A cup is a unit of volume: it measures how much space something takes up. A gram is a unit of mass: it measures how much something weighs. Converting between the two needs one more piece of information — how much the ingredient weighs for a given volume, which bakers call its density. A cup of water weighs 237 grams, because water is dense. A cup of all-purpose flour weighs about half that, because flour is a fine powder full of tiny pockets of air. A cup of honey weighs about 340 grams, nearly three times as much as the same cup of flour, because honey is far denser than either.

That is why any converter has to ask which ingredient you mean before it can give you a number. It is also why "how many grams in a cup" is a question with a different answer for every ingredient — and why guessing the wrong one quietly wrecks a recipe. If a recipe calls for a cup of flour and you weigh out 200 grams, the amount a cup of sugar would weigh, you have added two-thirds too much flour, and the result will be dry and heavy.

Why do sources disagree about flour — 120 g or 125 g?

If you check three baking charts, you will find three figures for a cup of flour: 120, 125, and sometimes 130 grams or more. None of them is wrong. They disagree because a cup of flour is not a fixed thing — it depends on how the flour gets into the cup.

King Arthur Baking's figure, 120 grams, assumes the flour is spooned into the cup and levelled off with a knife. The USDA figure, 125 grams, comes from its own cup study, which fills the cup a little more firmly. A cook who dips the cup straight into the flour bag and scoops will pack far more in — baking writers who have tested this report that a scooped cup of flour can weigh anywhere from 110 grams to 160 grams depending on the pressure. That 50-gram spread is the difference between a light sponge and a brick.

For everyday baking the difference between 120 and 125 grams does not matter: it is about 4 percent, smaller than the difference between one brand of flour and another. What matters is consistency — pick one figure and one filling method and use them every time. This guide and the converter both use the spooned-and-levelled figure of 120 grams, because it is the standard most modern cookbooks use, and because it is the method you can repeat most reliably at home.

How to convert cups to grams: the formula

The conversion is one multiplication:

Grams = cups × grams per cup for that ingredient

Take the number of cups, multiply by the weight of one cup of that ingredient from the chart above, and you have the weight in grams. A few examples, using the converter's figures:

  • 2 cups of all-purpose flour: 2 × 120 = 240 grams.
  • 1½ cups of granulated sugar: 1.5 × 200 = 300 grams.
  • ¾ cup of butter: 0.75 × 227 = 170 grams (170.25, rounded down).
  • ⅓ cup of honey: ⅓ × 340 = 113 grams (113.3, rounded down).
  • 2 cups of rolled oats: 2 × 90 = 180 grams.
  • 2 cups of uncooked rice: 2 × 185 = 370 grams.

To go the other way — grams back to cups — divide instead:

Cups = grams ÷ grams per cup for that ingredient

So 500 grams of flour is 500 ÷ 120 = 4.17 cups, which is about 4 cups plus 2¾ tablespoons (the leftover 0.17 of a cup is about 2.7 tablespoons). And 250 grams of butter is 250 ÷ 227 = 1.10 cups, just over one cup — add about one and a half tablespoons to a full cup. A recipe that calls for 100 grams of flour needs 100 ÷ 120 = 0.83 of a cup, which is about 13 tablespoons — a useful figure when you have no scale and no measuring cup either.

Tablespoons and teaspoons to grams

Small amounts are usually measured in spoons, not cups, and the same rule applies: one spoon of flour weighs far less than one spoon of sugar. One US cup is 16 tablespoons and 48 teaspoons, so the weight per spoon is the per-cup weight divided by 16 or 48. Salt and leavening deserve a warning. A tablespoon of table salt weighs 18 grams — more than twice a tablespoon of flour — so a spoon-based mistake with salt is a big one. And baking powder and baking soda are measured in teaspoons for a reason: one teaspoon of either weighs about 4.6 grams, and adding an extra teaspoon to a small batch of muffins can leave them tasting metallic and soapy. Weigh the salt and the leavening if you can; if you measure them with spoons, level them off carefully and never heap them.

Why does a cup of flour weigh different amounts?

Four things change how much a cup of flour weighs, and together they explain the whole 110-to-160-gram range:

How you fill the cup. This is the biggest factor by far. Scooping the cup through the flour bag compresses the flour and can add 30 to 40 grams to a cup. Spooning the flour in loosely and levelling it off keeps it near 120 grams. Sifting the flour first fluffs it up and can drop a cup below 110 grams.

The type of flour. Bread flour and all-purpose flour are close, at 120 to 127 grams a cup. Cake flour is finer and lighter, at about 114 grams. Whole wheat flour sits around 113 to 120 grams depending on the source. Almond flour is lighter still, at about 96 grams a cup. The chart gives the figures for the common types; for anything unusual, check the converter, which lists each ingredient's own weight.

How the flour is stored. Flour absorbs moisture from the air. A bag stored in a humid kitchen weighs more per cup than the same flour stored somewhere dry, and a bag that has been sitting under other bags in a cupboard will be more compacted than a freshly opened one. The effect is small — a few grams — but it is real, and it is one more reason weighing beats scooping.

Whether it is sifted. Sifting breaks up lumps and fills the flour with air. A cup of sifted flour weighs noticeably less than a cup of unsifted flour from the same bag. If a recipe says "1 cup sifted flour", measure the cup after sifting, not before — the order of the words is the instruction.

The practical lesson is simple: if a recipe's success depends on the flour — and for cakes, bread and pastry it always does — weigh the flour instead of measuring it in cups. A kitchen scale costs less than a ruined cake, and it removes all four variables at once.

US cup, metric cup and UK cup — they are not the same

There is more than one "cup". The cup in an American recipe is the US customary cup, which holds 236.59 millilitres. The metric cup used in Australia, New Zealand and much of the rest of the world holds 250 millilitres. The old UK imperial cup held 284 millilitres, although it is rarely used in modern British recipes, which mostly use grams or millilitres directly.

The difference between the US cup and the metric cup is about 5.7 percent: to turn a US-cup weight into a metric-cup weight, multiply by 1.06. So a metric cup of all-purpose flour weighs about 127 grams (120 × 1.06 = 126.8), a metric cup of sugar weighs about 211 grams (200 × 1.06), and a metric cup of butter weighs about 240 grams (227 × 1.06). If your measuring cup is marked 250 mL, use the metric figures; if it is marked 236 mL or says "US cup", use the figures in the chart above. For most home baking the difference is small enough to ignore — but if you are baking at scale or following a recipe that depends on exact ratios, it is worth knowing which cup you own.

How to measure a cup of flour without a scale

No scale, no problem — but measure properly, because the method is the whole point. The spoon-and-level method is the one King Arthur and most modern cookbooks recommend:

  1. Fluff the flour in its bag or container with a spoon, so it loosens up.
  2. Spoon the flour into the measuring cup until it heaps above the rim. Do not dip the cup into the bag and do not shake the cup to settle it.
  3. Level the top off with the straight edge of a knife, sweeping the excess back into the bag.

That gives you a cup of about 120 grams. The common mistake is the scoop: dipping the cup into the bag and levelling it off packs the flour down and can add 30 to 40 grams — a quarter of a cup extra, hidden in the same volume. If your cakes keep coming out dry and dense, the scoop is the first suspect.

For liquids, use a liquid measuring jug, not a dry cup: fill it to the line at eye level, on a flat surface. For butter, the wrapper does the work for you — in the US, butter is sold in sticks marked in tablespoons, and two sticks make a cup. For sticky ingredients like honey, lightly oil the cup or spoon first and the honey will slide out instead of clinging to it.

Scaling a recipe up or down

Grams make scaling simple, because every quantity is a plain number. Say a cake recipe makes one cake and you want one and a half cakes. Convert each line to grams first, then multiply by 1.5:

Original (cups)Grams× 1.5
2 cups flour240 g360 g
1½ cups sugar300 g450 g
½ cup butter114 g170 g
¾ cup milk184 g276 g

Check each line against the formula — grams = cups × grams per cup. Two cups of flour is 2 × 120 = 240 grams, and × 1.5 is 360 grams. One and a half cups of sugar is 1.5 × 200 = 300 grams, and × 1.5 is 450 grams. Half a cup of butter is 0.5 × 227 = 113.5 grams — call it 114 — and × 1.5 is 170 grams. Three-quarters of a cup of milk is 0.75 × 245 = 183.75 grams — call it 184 — and × 1.5 is 276 grams. Convert the recipe to grams once, write the gram weights on the recipe card, and scaling it ever again is one multiplication per line.

Common mistakes when converting cups to grams

Using one number for every ingredient. A cup is not 200 grams, or 120 grams, or any single number. It is 120 grams of flour, 200 grams of sugar and 227 grams of butter. The ingredient is part of the conversion, not an afterthought.

Scooping the flour. Dipping the cup into the bag packs the flour and adds up to 40 grams a cup. Spoon and level instead, or weigh it.

Packing brown sugar wrong. Brown sugar is the one ingredient that is meant to be packed: press it firmly into the cup until it holds its shape when turned out. A loosely filled cup of brown sugar can be 30 grams light. The chart's 220 grams assumes packed sugar — if you do not pack it, expect about 190 grams.

Measuring dry ingredients in a liquid jug. A liquid jug is hard to level off, so dry ingredients measured in one are usually overfilled. Use a dry cup you can sweep level with a knife.

Mixing up US and metric cups. A recipe written for US cups and measured with a 250 mL metric cup adds about 6 percent to every ingredient. Small enough to ignore for a stew, big enough to notice in a sponge cake.

Forgetting that sifted flour is lighter. If the recipe says "1 cup sifted flour", sift first and then measure. Measuring first and sifting after gives you more flour than the recipe intends.

Frequently asked questions

How many grams is 1 cup of flour?

About 120 grams for all-purpose flour, spooned into the cup and levelled off — the figure King Arthur Baking publishes and the one this guide and the converter use. The USDA lists 125 grams, because it fills the cup slightly more firmly. A scooped cup can weigh up to 160 grams.

How many grams is 1 cup of sugar?

About 200 grams for granulated white sugar. Brown sugar, packed firmly into the cup, weighs about 220 grams; powdered sugar, unsifted, weighs about 120 grams.

How many grams is 1 cup of butter?

227 grams — almost exactly half a pound. A cup of butter is two sticks, and one stick (half a cup) weighs 113 grams. One tablespoon of butter weighs 14.2 grams.

How many grams is 2 cups of flour?

240 grams (2 × 120 g). Two cups of sugar is 400 grams, and two cups of butter is 454 grams.

Is a cup 250 grams?

No. A cup is a volume, not a weight, so it has no single weight in grams. A metric cup of water weighs about 250 grams because a metric cup holds 250 mL and water weighs 1 gram per mL — but a US cup of flour weighs about 120 grams and a US cup of honey about 340 grams.

How many cups is 500 grams of flour?

About 4 cups plus 2½ tablespoons: 500 ÷ 120 = 4.17 cups. For sugar, 500 grams is 2½ cups exactly (500 ÷ 200 = 2.5).

How many grams is a cup of oats?

About 90 grams for rolled oats. Oats are light and bulky, so a cup weighs less than a cup of flour — and the figure varies a little by brand and by how finely the oats are rolled.

Do I need a kitchen scale to bake well?

Not for every recipe, but for cakes, bread and pastry a scale is the single biggest improvement you can make for the least money. It removes the filling method, the cup size and the humidity from the equation entirely: 120 grams of flour is 120 grams of flour, however it got into the bowl.
