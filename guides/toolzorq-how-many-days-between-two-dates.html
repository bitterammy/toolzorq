How Many Days Are There Between Two Dates?

Short answer: subtract the start date from the end date. From 10 November 2026 to 25 December 2026 is 45 days. The start day itself is not counted — you are measuring the gap between the two dates, not the number of dates you touch. If you want to count both end dates as well, for example the total days of a holiday, add 1 to the result.

It sounds like a question a child could answer, and for two dates in the same week it is. But stretch the gap across months and the counting gets awkward fast: months have 28, 29, 30 or 31 days, February changes length every four years, and it is easy to be off by one without ever noticing. A deadline that is really 45 days away feels like 46, a holiday that is 14 days long gets booked as 13, and a project plan quietly slips by a day. This guide explains the one rule that settles every case, works through real examples you can check, and shows how to get the answer in seconds with TOOLZORQ's Date Calculator, in a spreadsheet, or on a paper calendar.

The one rule: subtract, do not count on your fingers

The number of days between two dates is the end date minus the start date, counted in whole calendar days. That is the rule every calculator, spreadsheet and calendar app uses, and it is the rule behind every example in this guide. You do not count the start date; you count the days that pass after it until you reach the end date. Think of it like measuring the distance between two fence posts: the gap is what you measure, not the posts themselves.

Take a small example first. From 1 January to 10 January is 9 days, not 10. Count forward from 1 January: 2 January is day 1, 3 January is day 2, and so on until 10 January, which is day 9. The start date, 1 January, is day zero. This is the single most common source of error, and it is worth fixing in your head before anything else: when someone asks "how many days between", they almost always mean this gap, and the answer excludes the start date.

The same rule works across any distance. From 1 January 2026 to 1 January 2027 is 365 days, because 2026 is not a leap year. From 1 January 2024 to 1 January 2025 is 366 days, because 2024 is a leap year and 29 February 2024 falls inside the gap. From 10 November 2026 to 25 December 2026 is 45 days — six weeks and three days. And from 10 November 2026 to 1 January 2027 is 52 days. None of these needs a calendar once you know the rule; it only needs the subtraction, which is exactly what a calculator does for you.

There is a second, equally useful way to think about it. The gap between two dates is the number of midnights you cross going from the first date to the second. From 1 January to 10 January you cross nine midnights. From 10 November to 25 December you cross forty-five. If you can picture the midnights, you can picture why the start date does not count: at the start date itself, no midnight has been crossed yet.

When the answer is off by one: inclusive counting

Most "wrong" answers to this question are not wrong arithmetic — they are a different question. The gap between two dates and the number of dates in a range are two different numbers, and they differ by exactly one. The gap from 1 January to 10 January is 9 days. The number of dates from 1 January to 10 January inclusive — 1st, 2nd, 3rd, all the way to 10th — is 10. Both numbers are correct; they answer different questions.

Which one you want depends on what you are counting. Use the gap, the plain subtraction, when you are measuring time passing: how many days until a deadline, how many days since an event, how long a project phase lasts. Use the inclusive count, the gap plus one, when you are counting days you will occupy or experience: the days of a holiday, the days of a course, the days of a rental, the days of a sick leave. A holiday from 10 November to 25 December is 45 days away at its start and lasts 46 days inclusive — and confusing the two is how a 14-night trip gets booked as 13 nights.

The rule of thumb is simple. If the question has the word "between" or "until" in it, use the gap. If it has the word "from … to … inclusive", or you are counting days you will be somewhere, add one. And if someone else gives you a number, ask which one they meant: half of all date arguments are two people answering different questions with the same words.

Leap years and month lengths

Two facts about the calendar cause almost every hand-counting error. The first is that months are not all the same length: January, March, May, July, August, October and December have 31 days; April, June, September and November have 30; February has 28 in an ordinary year and 29 in a leap year. The old knuckle mnemonic still works — the raised knuckles are the 31-day months — but in practice, nobody should be counting month lengths by hand when a calculator exists.

The second fact is the leap-year rule. A year is a leap year if it divides evenly by 4, except that years divisible by 100 are not leap years unless they are also divisible by 400. So 2024 and 2028 are leap years, 2026 is not, 1900 was not a leap year, and 2000 was. The rule exists because a year is not exactly 365 days long: the Earth takes about 365.25 days to orbit the Sun, and the extra quarter-day is corrected by adding a day every four years, with the century exceptions keeping the calendar aligned over the long term.

For counting days between dates, only one question matters: does 29 February fall inside the gap? If it does, the gap is one day longer than the same dates in a non-leap year. From 1 March 2026 to 1 March 2027 is 365 days; from 1 March 2027 to 1 March 2028 is 366 days, because 29 February 2028 is inside the second gap. If both dates are before 29 February, or both after it, the leap day does not affect the count at all.

Calendar days are not business days

A second distinction that causes real mistakes is between calendar days and business days. A calendar day is every day on the calendar, weekends included. A business day is usually a Monday-to-Friday day, excluding weekends and public holidays. The same two dates can give very different answers depending on which one you mean.

From 10 November 2026, a Tuesday, to 25 December 2026, a Friday, is 45 calendar days. Count only the Mondays to Fridays in that span, excluding the start date and including the end date, and the answer is 33 business days. The difference — twelve weekend days — is large enough to break a delivery promise or a project plan if the wrong count is used. Deadlines in contracts and notices usually mean calendar days unless they say "business days" or "working days", so read the wording before you count, and if the wording is unclear, ask. A deadline of "30 days" and a deadline of "30 business days" are about six weeks apart.

Public holidays make business-day counting harder still, because they vary by country and by region, and a calculator that does not know your holidays will get the number wrong. Counting forward or backwards from a date

The reverse question is just as common: what date is 90 days from today, or 30 days before a deadline? This is the same subtraction run in the other direction, and it is where hand-counting fails most often, because adding days across month boundaries means keeping track of how long each month is as you go.

From Tuesday 10 November 2026, adding 90 days lands on Monday 8 February 2027. Adding 30 days lands on Thursday 10 December 2026. Going backwards, 30 days before 10 November 2026 is Sunday 11 October 2026, and 365 days after 10 November 2026 is Wednesday 10 November 2027. Check the 90-day example by hand: 20 days remain in November after the 10th, December adds 31, January adds 31, which brings the total to 82, and 8 more days into February reaches the 8th — 20 + 31 + 31 + 8 = 90. It works, but it is slow, and one wrong month length ruins the answer.

How to count days in Excel and Google Sheets

If the dates already live in a spreadsheet, you do not need a separate tool at all: spreadsheets store dates as numbers, so subtracting two dates gives the days between them directly. Put the start date in cell A2 and the end date in cell B2, and in another cell type =B2-A2. The result is the gap in days, with the start date not counted — the same rule as everywhere else in this guide. Make sure both cells are formatted as dates, or the subtraction will produce a number that looks like a date and confuse you.

Two dedicated functions say the same thing more explicitly. =DAYS(B2,A2) returns the end date minus the start date directly, and =DATEDIF(A2,B2,"d") returns the same gap in days. DATEDIF can also return the gap in months with "m" or years with "y", which is handy when you want "1 year, 1 month and 22 days" rather than a single number of days. Add 1 to any of these results to count both end dates.

For adding days to a date in a spreadsheet, the arithmetic is just as simple: a date plus a number is a date that many days later, so =A2+90 gives the date 90 days after the date in A2, and =A2-30 gives the date 30 days before it. Format the result cell as a date, or you will see a serial number instead of a calendar date. In Google Sheets, the same formulas work identically.

Counting on a paper calendar or your phone

Sometimes there is no tool at hand, and the old methods still work. On a paper calendar, count the days left in the first month after the start date, add the whole months in between, then add the days into the last month — the same month-by-month method used in the worked examples above. Mark the start date and the end date with a pen first, so you can see the span, and count the marked start date as zero.

On a phone, the calendar app can do the job without any maths. In most calendar apps, you can long-press a date to see its details, and some show the number of days between two selected dates. The simplest phone method is to create an event on the start date and another on the end date, then look at the gap — or just open the Date Calculator in the phone's browser, which works the same as on a computer and needs nothing installed.

Whichever method you use, the check is the same: try it on a pair of dates you already know. If your method says 1 January to 10 January is 9 days, it is counting the gap correctly. If it says 10, it is counting both end dates, and you need to subtract one for a "days between" answer.

Common mistakes when counting days

Counting the start date. The most common error, and the one behind most off-by-one answers. The start date is day zero, not day one. From 1 January to 10 January is 9 days.

Forgetting February. February has 28 days, or 29 in a leap year, and a gap that crosses February is one day shorter than a gap that crosses a 31-day month. A 30-day month crossed by mistake as 31 days adds a phantom day to the answer.

Forgetting the leap day. A gap that contains 29 February is one day longer than the same dates in a non-leap year. From 1 March 2027 to 1 March 2028 is 366 days, not 365.

Mixing up calendar days and business days. A "30-day" deadline is about six weeks in business days. Read whether the deadline means calendar days or business days before you count, and never assume.

Counting both end dates when you meant the gap. A holiday from 10 November to 25 December lasts 46 days inclusive, but it starts in 45 days. Know which number you need before you count.

Trusting a rough "a month is 30 days" estimate. A month is 28, 29, 30 or 31 days, and three months is 89, 90, 91 or 92 days depending on which months they are. Estimates are fine for planning; they are not fine for deadlines.

Frequently asked questions

How many days are there between two dates?

Subtract the start date from the end date. From 10 November 2026 to 25 December 2026 is 45 days. The start date is not counted — you are measuring the gap between the dates. Add 1 if you want to count both end dates as well.

How do I count the days between two dates in Excel?

Put the start date in A2 and the end date in B2, then use =B2-A2. The result is the gap in days. =DAYS(B2,A2) and =DATEDIF(A2,B2,"d") give the same answer. Add 1 to count both end dates.

Does the count include the start and end dates?

By default, no: the standard count is the gap, which excludes the start date and includes the end date. If you want to count both end dates — for a holiday, a course or a period of leave — add 1 to the result.
